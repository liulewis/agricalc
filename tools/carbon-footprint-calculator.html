Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my farm's Carbon Footprint?

Calculate farm Carbon Footprint by summing emissions from all sources: livestock enteric fermentation (cattle produce 100-200 kg CH4/head/year), manure management (CH4 and N2O from storage), fertilizer application (1-2% of applied N becomes N2O — 1 kg N2O = 298 kg CO2e), fuel combustion (diesel 2.68 kg CO2/L, gasoline 2.32 kg CO2/L), electricity use (0.4-0.8 kg CO2/kWh depending on grid), and land use change. Use GHG Protocol or IPCC guidelines. Carbon sequestration from soil (cover crops, no-till can sequester 0.2-2 t CO2e/ha/year) and trees offsets emissions. Net footprint = total emissions − sequestration. Many farms can reduce footprint 20-40% through improved management.

What are the biggest sources of Carbon Footprint in agriculture?

Biggest agricultural Carbon Footprint sources globally: enteric fermentation from ruminants (≈40% of ag emissions — cattle, sheep, goats produce methane during digestion), manure management (≈15% — methane from anaerobic storage, nitrous oxide from decomposition), synthetic fertilizer use (≈15% — nitrous oxide from soil after N application), rice cultivation (≈10% — methane from flooded paddies), and fuel/energy use (≈10%). In crop-only farms, fertilizer and fuel dominate. In livestock farms, enteric fermentation dominates. Reducing these: improve feed efficiency (less methane per kg product), anaerobic digesters for manure, precision fertilization, alternate wetting/drying for rice, renewable energy.

How can I reduce my farm's Carbon Footprint?

Reduce Carbon Footprint through: soil carbon sequestration (cover crops, no-till/reduced tillage, crop rotation — sequesters 0.2-2 t CO2e/ha/year), livestock management (improve feed efficiency, add 3-NOP or seaweed to reduce methane 20-80%, manure composting vs lagoon storage), nutrient management (split N applications, use inhibitors, soil testing to avoid over-application — reduces N2O), renewable energy (solar panels, biogas from manure, wind), energy efficiency (LED lighting, variable speed motors, insulation), and agroforestry (trees sequester 5-20 t CO2e/ha/year). Many reductions also save money — less fertilizer, less fuel, lower energy bills.

Can I earn carbon credits for Carbon Footprint reduction?

Yes, farmers can earn carbon credits through verified protocols: Verra VCS, Gold Standard, Climate Action Reserve, and USDA climate-smart programs. Common activities: no-till conversion (0.2-0.5 t CO2e/acre/year), cover cropping (0.2-1.0 t CO2e/acre/year), improved grazing management (0.5-2 t CO2e/acre/year), anaerobic digesters (large volume credits), afforestation/reforestation. Credit prices: $5-30/t CO2e (voluntary market), higher for verified additionality. Requirements: baseline measurement, third-party verification, permanence commitments (20-100 years), leakage monitoring. Transaction costs can be high for small farms — group aggregation through cooperatives reduces per-farm costs. Revenue is typically $5-50/acre/year.

How accurate are Carbon Footprint calculations?

Carbon Footprint calculations use IPCC emission factors which have uncertainty ranges: enteric fermentation ±20-30%, manure management ±30-50%, fertilizer N2O ±40-60%, fuel combustion ±5-10%. Tier 1 (default factors) is least accurate but simplest; Tier 2 (country/region-specific factors) improves accuracy; Tier 3 (farm-specific measurement, direct monitoring) is most accurate but expensive. For most farms, Tier 1-2 provides a reasonable estimate for tracking trends. Key accuracy tips: use actual fuel/fertilizer records rather than estimates, measure manure management system type, use local climate data. The goal is identifying major sources and tracking reductions over time, not laboratory precision.
