Calculating Livestock Weight Gain and Feed Conversion

10 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 7 min read

Introduction

Weight gain and feed conversion are the two metrics that determine whether a growing or finishing enterprise makes money. Average Daily Gain (ADG) tells you how fast animals are growing; the Feed Conversion Ratio (FCR) tells you how efficiently they turn feed into meat. Together with feed price, they produce the cost of gain — the dollars it costs to put on each pound or kilogram of live weight. This is the number that decides whether a feeder calf purchase, a pig finishing batch, or a broiler flock is profitable.

This article defines each metric with the exact formulas, provides species-specific benchmark ranges from published research and industry data, and walks through a complete cattle backgrounding example. We also cover the most frequent errors producers make when interpreting these numbers.

Livestock Weight Gain Calculator: Average Daily Gain

ADG is the simplest growth metric: total weight gained divided by days on feed.

ADG (lb/day) = (Ending weight − Starting weight) ÷ Days on feed

Benchmark ADG ranges by species and production phase:

  • Beef cattle backgrounding (weaning to 800 lb): 1.5–2.5 lb/day on forage-based diets; 2.5–3.5 lb/day on high-grain growing rations.
  • Beef finishing (800 lb to slaughter): 3.0–4.0 lb/day on high-concentrate rations; exceptional pens can reach 4.5 lb/day.
  • Growing-finishing pigs (50–250 lb): 1.5–2.0 lb/day; modern genetics with ad libitum feeding can exceed 2.2 lb/day.
  • Broiler chickens (0–6 weeks): 0.08–0.12 lb/day (40–55 g/day); a 6-week broiler reaches 5.5–6.5 lb.
  • Dairy heifers (pre-breeding to calving): 1.5–2.0 lb/day, targeting age at first calving of 22–24 months.

ADG should be measured with consistent scale conditions — same time of day, similar gut fill, calibrated scales. Weighing after a long haul or after withholding feed overnight will depress starting weights and inflate apparent ADG.

Feed Conversion Ratio Calculator: Livestock FCR

FCR is the amount of feed consumed per unit of weight gained. Lower is better.

FCR = Total feed consumed (as-fed or DM) ÷ Total weight gained

Always specify whether feed is measured on an as-fed or dry matter (DM) basis — the two are not comparable. Industry convention varies: cattle FCR is usually reported on a DM basis, while swine and poultry FCR is typically as-fed.

Benchmark FCR ranges:

  • Beef finishing: 5.5–7.5 lb DM per lb gain (as-fed roughly 6–8 lb/lb). Cattle on high-forage growing diets may be 8–12:1.
  • Growing-finishing pigs: 2.4–3.0 lb feed per lb gain (as-fed). Modern high-health herds achieve 2.4–2.6:1.
  • Broilers: 1.5–1.9 lb feed per lb gain. Industry leaders are below 1.6:1 at 42 days.
  • Turkeys: 2.2–2.8:1 to market weight.
  • Lambs finishing: 4.0–6.0:1 on concentrate diets.

FCR worsens as animals get heavier and closer to mature weight — maintenance requirements consume a larger share of intake. This is why finishing enterprises watch the marginal FCR closely to know when to ship.

Worked Example: 60-Head Cattle Backgrounding Pen

Scenario: 60 steers starting at 550 lb, fed a growing ration (55% forage, 45% concentrate) for 120 days, ending at 890 lb. Average daily DMI is 18.5 lb/head. Feed cost (DM basis) is $0.095/lb.

Step 1 — ADG: (890 − 550) ÷ 120 = 2.83 lb/day.
Step 2 — Total gain per head: 2.83 × 120 = 340 lb.
Step 3 — Total feed per head (DM): 18.5 × 120 = 2,220 lb.
Step 4 — FCR (DM): 2,220 ÷ 340 = 6.53:1.
Step 5 — Feed cost per head: 2,220 × $0.095 = $210.90.
Step 6 — Cost of gain: $210.90 ÷ 340 = $0.62/lb gain.

For the full pen: total gain = 340 × 60 = 20,400 lb; total feed cost = $12,654. If feeder calves cost $1.80/lb at 550 lb ($990/head) and finished cattle sell at $1.55/lb at 890 lb ($1,379.50/head), the gross margin per head is $1,379.50 − $990 − $210.90 = $178.60, or $10,716 for the pen — before yardage, veterinary, and death loss.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Mixing as-fed and DM FCR. A pig FCR of 2.6:1 (as-fed) and a cattle FCR of 6.5:1 (DM) look wildly different, but both are normal for their species. Always label the basis.

2. Ignoring maintenance energy. FCR includes both growth and maintenance. A cold winter can increase maintenance requirements by 10–20%, worsening FCR even if intake and gain look stable. Provide windbreaks and bedding to mitigate.

3. Inconsistent weighing. Weighing at different times of day or with different gut fill creates phantom gains or losses of 10–20 lb per head. Standardize: weigh before morning feeding, after a 2-hour shrink, same scale.

4. Not tracking marginal FCR. Overall pen FCR hides the point at which additional days on feed become unprofitable. Calculate FCR for the last 28–30 days separately; when marginal FCR exceeds 8–9:1 in cattle, it is usually time to market.

5. Confusing FCR with feed efficiency. Some industries report feed efficiency as gain ÷ feed (the inverse of FCR). A feed efficiency of 0.16 is equivalent to an FCR of 6.25:1. Check which convention your buyer or consultant uses.

Practical Case Study: Real-World Application

Consider a 50-hectare mixed farm in the Midwest United States implementing calculating livestock weight gain and feed conversion. In practice, this means conducting a baseline assessment during the off-season, identifying the top two priority areas for improvement, and allocating a small trial area (2–5% of total acreage) to test new practices before full-scale implementation. Documenting inputs, costs, and outcomes on the trial plot provides actionable data for future decisions.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

ADG, FCR, and cost of gain form the core financial dashboard for any growing livestock enterprise. For cattle backgrounding, target ADG of 2.5–3.0 lb/day and FCR of 6–7:1 (DM); for finishing pigs, 1.8–2.0 lb/day and 2.5–2.8:1; for broilers, 50+ g/day and under 1.7:1. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good feed conversion ratio for cattle?

Beef cattle on finishing rations typically achieve 5.5–7.5 lb dry matter per pound of gain. Growing cattle on forage-based diets may range from 8–12:1.

How do I calculate average daily gain?

ADG = (ending weight − starting weight) ÷ days on feed. Weigh under consistent conditions to avoid gut-fill errors.

What is cost of gain and why does it matter?

Cost of gain = total feed cost ÷ total weight gained, expressed as $/lb or €/kg. It tells you exactly what each additional pound of animal costs to produce and is the key metric for deciding when to buy, sell, or hold.

Does FCR change as animals get heavier?

Yes. FCR worsens (increases) as animals approach mature weight because maintenance energy consumes a larger share of total intake. This is why finishing enterprises track marginal FCR to time marketing.

What is a good FCR for broilers?

Modern broiler flocks achieve 1.5–1.9 lb of feed per pound of gain at 42 days. High-health, well-managed flocks can reach 1.5–1.6:1.
