Cocoa Fertilizer Management: NPK Rates for West African Farms

29 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 11 min read

Related Calculators: cocoa fertilizer calculator · NPK fertilizer calculator This guide covers cocoa fertilizer application rate calculator in detail.

Introduction

West Africa produces more than 70% of the world's cocoa, with Ivory Coast (45%), Ghana (18%), and Nigeria (6%) dominating global supply. Yet average yields in the region remain at 400–600 kg/ha, far below the 1,500–2,000 kg/ha achievable under good management. A primary cause is chronic nutrient mining: cocoa trees remove significant quantities of N, P, K, Ca, Mg, and S in each pod harvest, and most smallholder farmers apply little or no fertilizer. Over decades, soils become depleted, tree health declines, and yields collapse.

This article provides evidence-based fertilizer recommendations for cocoa in West Africa, covering nutrient removal rates, optimal NPK rates by tree age, split application timing aligned with the bimodal rainfall pattern, leaf nutrient diagnostic standards, and worked examples for Ivory Coast and Ghana. We also address the specific role of potassium and magnesium, which are often the most limiting nutrients in cocoa systems.

Cocoa Fertilizer Calculator: Nutrient Removal and Requirements

Each tonne of dry cocoa beans removes approximately the following nutrients from the field (based on studies by ICCO and CRIG):

  • Nitrogen (N): 25–30 kg
  • Phosphorus (P): 5–7 kg (as P, or 11–16 kg P₂O₅)
  • Potassium (K): 35–45 kg (as K, or 42–54 kg K₂O)
  • Calcium (Ca): 5–8 kg
  • Magnesium (Mg): 4–6 kg (as Mg, or 7–10 kg MgO)
  • Sulfur (S): 3–5 kg

At a yield of 1,000 kg/ha (1 tonne), the crop removes roughly 25–30 kg N, 12 kg P₂O₅, and 45 kg K₂O. But fertilizer recommendations must exceed removal to account for nutrient use efficiency (typically 30–50% for N, 10–20% for P, 40–60% for K in tropical soils), leaching losses in high-rainfall environments, and the needs of tree vegetative growth.

Recommended annual nutrient rates for mature cocoa (8+ years) in West Africa:

  • N: 75–125 kg/ha/year (lower on fertile forest soils, higher on degraded savanna-transition soils)
  • P₂O₅: 30–60 kg/ha/year
  • K₂O: 75–150 kg/ha/year (K is critical for bean filling and disease resistance)
  • MgO: 15–30 kg/ha/year (often deficient in highly weathered soils; apply as dolomitic lime or kieserite)
  • Ca: 50–100 kg/ha/year via liming if soil pH < 5.5

Cocoa Fertilizer Management: Formulation and Timing

The most commonly used compound fertilizer for cocoa in West Africa is a 0-23-19 + 9CaO + 8MgO + 7S blend (often called "cocoa fertilizer" or "Asaase Wura" in Ghana), applied at 375–500 kg/ha/year, supplemented with urea or ammonium sulfate for additional N. Alternatively, a 12-15-15 or 14-14-14 compound at 300–400 kg/ha plus muriate of potash (KCl) at 100–150 kg/ha can be used.

Application timing follows the bimodal rainfall pattern (major rainy season April–July, minor September–November):

  • First application (late April–May): 50–60% of annual N, all P, and 40% of K — applied at the onset of major rains to support vegetative flushing and flowering.
  • Second application (August–September): 40–50% of N and 60% of K — applied before minor rains to support pod development and bean filling.
  • Lime/Mg: Apply dolomitic lime every 2–3 years at 1–2 t/ha, broadcast under canopy, at the beginning of the dry season (December–January).

Apply fertilizer in a ring or band 30–50 cm from the trunk, under the canopy drip line, not against the trunk (which can burn the bark and roots). Incorporate lightly or let rainfall wash it in. Avoid applying when the soil is dry — fertilizer salts can damage roots without moisture to dissolve and move them.

Worked Example 1: 5-Ha Cocoa Farm, Ashanti Region, Ghana

Farm: 5 hectares, mature hybrid cocoa (10 years old), 1,000 trees/ha (3 m × 3 m spacing), current yield 600 kg/ha, target 1,000 kg/ha. Soil: ochrosol (forest oxisol), pH 5.8, low K and Mg. Leaf analysis: N 2.1% (adequate), P 0.18% (low), K 1.2% (deficient), Mg 0.25% (deficient).

Recommended nutrient rates: N 100 kg/ha, P₂O₅ 45 kg/ha, K₂O 120 kg/ha, MgO 25 kg/ha.

Fertilizer program:
Compound 0-23-19 + 8MgO at 400 kg/ha: provides P₂O₅ 92 kg, K₂O 76 kg, MgO 32 kg — covers P and Mg, partially covers K.
Supplement K: KCl (60% K₂O) at 75 kg/ha: adds K₂O 45 kg → total K₂O 121 kg/ha ✓.
Supplement N: Urea (46% N) at 217 kg/ha: provides N 100 kg/ha ✓.
Total fertilizer: 400 + 75 + 217 = 692 kg/ha × 5 ha = 3,460 kg.

Split application:
May: 130 kg/ha urea + 400 kg/ha compound + 30 kg/ha KCl (500 kg/ha total).
September: 87 kg/ha urea + 45 kg/ha KCl (132 kg/ha total).

Cost estimate: Compound $0.45/kg × 400 = $180; Urea $0.40/kg × 217 = $87; KCl $0.38/kg × 75 = $29. Total = $296/ha × 5 = $1,480/year. Expected yield increase from 600 to 1,000 kg/ha = 400 kg × $2.50/kg (farm gate) = $1,000/ha × 5 = $5,000 additional revenue. ROI = 238% in year 1.

Worked Example 2: Young Cocoa Farm, Soubré Region, Ivory Coast

Farm: 3 hectares, 4-year-old cocoa (pre-bearing to early bearing), 1,100 trees/ha (3 m × 3 m with some replacement). Soil: highly weathered ferralsol, pH 5.2, low Ca and Mg. Target: establish strong framework for production at year 5–6.

Young tree rates (years 1–4): N 50–75 kg/ha, P₂O₅ 25–40 kg/ha, K₂O 40–60 kg/ha, plus liming.

Program:
Year 1–2: Compound 12-15-15 at 200 g/tree/year (220 kg/ha), split into 4 applications every 3 months during rains. Plus dolomitic lime at 1.5 t/ha in year 1.
Year 3–4: Increase to 300 g/tree/year of 12-15-15 (330 kg/ha), split into 2 applications (May and September). Plus KCl at 50 g/tree (55 kg/ha) in September.
Year 5+: Transition to mature tree rates.

For young trees, apply fertilizer in a 20-cm ring around the base, gradually expanding to the drip line as the canopy grows. Avoid contact with the stem.

Leaf Nutrient Diagnostic Standards

Leaf analysis is the most reliable way to diagnose nutrient deficiencies in cocoa. Sample the 3rd–4th mature leaf from the top of non-bearing shoots, 30–40 leaves per field, during the main flushing period (May–June). Optimal ranges (CRIG/ICCO standards):

  • N: 2.0–2.5% (below 1.8% = deficient)
  • P: 0.18–0.25% (below 0.15% = deficient)
  • K: 1.5–2.0% (below 1.3% = deficient; K deficiency is the most common in West Africa)
  • Ca: 0.8–1.5%
  • Mg: 0.30–0.50% (below 0.25% = deficient; causes yellowing between leaf veins)
  • Zn: 30–50 ppm
  • B: 25–50 ppm

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Applying fertilizer against the trunk. Fertilizer placed within 20 cm of the trunk can burn bark and surface roots, causing stem cankers. Apply at the drip line (30–50 cm from trunk for mature trees).

2. Ignoring potassium. K is the most yield-limiting nutrient in cocoa after N, and it is critical for bean size and black pod disease resistance. Many farmers use only NPK compound and forget supplemental K.

3. Applying in the dry season. Fertilizer needs rainfall to dissolve and move into the root zone. Applying on dry soil wastes fertilizer and can cause salt injury. Time applications to coincide with the onset of rains.

4. Not liming acidic soils. Most West African cocoa soils are acidic (pH 4.5–5.5), which reduces P availability and increases Al and Mn toxicity. Dolomitic lime every 2–3 years raises pH and supplies Ca and Mg.

5. Overapplying N. Excess N promotes excessive vegetative growth, reduces pod set, and increases susceptibility to black pod and capsid damage. Balance N with K — a K:N ratio of 1.2–1.5:1 is optimal for cocoa.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Cocoa fertilizer management in West Africa is a matter of replacing nutrients removed in the harvest and correcting the specific deficiencies of highly weathered tropical soils. For mature trees, target 75–125 kg N, 30–60 kg P₂O₅, 75–150 kg K₂O, and 15–30 kg MgO per hectare per year, split into two applications aligned with the bimodal rainfall pattern. Use leaf analysis to fine-tune rates, lime acidic soils, and always apply at the drip line rather than the trunk. A $300/ha fertilizer investment can increase yields from 600 to 1,000 kg/ha, generating $1,000/ha in additional revenue — one of the highest-ROI interventions available to West African cocoa farmers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much fertilizer does a cocoa tree need per year?

A mature cocoa tree needs approximately 75–125 g N, 30–60 g P₂O₅, and 75–150 g K₂O per year. At 1,000 trees/ha, this equals 75–125 kg N, 30–60 kg P₂O₅, and 75–150 kg K₂O per hectare.

What is the best fertilizer for cocoa?

A compound fertilizer with high P and K, such as 0-23-19 + MgO (cocoa fertilizer), supplemented with urea for N and KCl for additional K. Dolomitic lime should be applied every 2–3 years on acidic soils.

When should I apply fertilizer to cocoa?

Split into two applications: first in late April–May (onset of major rains) for vegetative growth and flowering; second in August–September (before minor rains) for pod development and bean filling.

What is the most common nutrient deficiency in cocoa?

Potassium (K) deficiency is the most widespread in West African cocoa farms, followed by magnesium (Mg). K deficiency reduces bean size and increases black pod susceptibility; Mg causes interveinal chlorosis.

How much can fertilizer increase cocoa yield?

Fertilizer application on depleted soils can increase yields from 400–600 kg/ha to 1,000–1,500 kg/ha — a 60–150% increase. The response is greatest when combined with improved varieties, shade management, and pest/disease control.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much fertilizer does a cocoa tree need per year?

A mature cocoa tree (5+ years) requires 75–150 g N, 20–40 g P₂O₅, and 100–200 g K₂O per year. At a typical planting density of 1,000–1,200 trees/ha, this equals 75–180 kg N, 20–48 kg P₂O₅, and 100–240 kg K₂O per hectare. Young trees (1–4 years) receive 25–50% of the mature rate. Use a cocoa fertilizer calculator to determine exact rates based on your soil test and yield target.

What is the best NPK ratio for cocoa?

Cocoa requires a balanced fertilizer with emphasis on potassium and nitrogen. A 15-5-20 or 14-6-24 NPK blend is commonly recommended, supplemented with KCl where soil potassium is low. Avoid high-phosphorus fertilizers as cocoa has relatively low P requirements. On acidic soils (pH < 5.5), apply dolomitic lime at 1–2 t/ha every 2–3 years to maintain pH 5.5–6.5 and supply calcium and magnesium.

When should I apply fertilizer to cocoa trees?

Split fertilizer into 2–3 applications per year. First application at the onset of the main rainy season (March–April in West Africa) to support vegetative growth and flowering. Second application mid-rainy season (June–July) to support pod development. In areas with two rainy seasons, a third light application can be made at the start of the short rains. Apply fertilizer in a ring 30–50 cm from the trunk, avoiding direct contact with the stem, and lightly incorporate into the soil.

How does fertilizer affect cocoa yield?

Proper fertilization can increase cocoa yields by 50–100% compared to unfertilized trees, especially on depleted soils. Nitrogen supports vegetative growth and photosynthesis; potassium is critical for pod filling and bean size (each 100 kg of dry beans removes approximately 2 kg N, 0.5 kg P, and 3 kg K); phosphorus supports root development and energy transfer. Yield response is typically 0.5–1.5 kg dry beans per kg of N applied, depending on soil fertility and other management factors. Leaf analysis every 1–2 years helps fine-tune fertilizer programs.
