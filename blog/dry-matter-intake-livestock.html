Dry Matter Intake for Livestock: How to Calculate and Optimize DMI

4 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 9 min read

Introduction

Dry matter intake (DMI) is the foundation of all livestock ration balancing. Every nutrient requirement — energy, protein, minerals — is expressed on a dry matter basis, and every feed ingredient has a variable moisture content that must be accounted for. A dairy nutritionist who ignores DMI will underfeed a high-producing cow; a beef producer who confuses as-fed with dry matter will overbuy hay by 15–30%.

This guide explains what DMI is, how to predict it using NRC equations and practical rules of thumb, how to convert between as-fed and dry matter, and how to use DMI to check whether a ration actually meets an animal's requirements. We include a complete dairy cow ration example and the most common errors.

Dry Matter Intake Calculator: What Is Dry Matter?

Dry matter is what remains after all water is removed from a feed. It contains all the energy, protein, fiber, and minerals the animal actually uses. Moisture content varies enormously: fresh pasture is 15–30% DM (70–85% water); hay is 85–92% DM; silage is 30–40% DM; grain is 86–90% DM. Two feeds that weigh the same on a scale can deliver very different amounts of actual nutrients.

All livestock nutrient requirements (NRC, INRA, AFRC) are published on a DM basis. If a 1,300-lb dairy cow needs 52 lb of DM per day and you feed her 60 lb of 50% DM silage, she is only getting 30 lb of DM — barely half her requirement. This is the most common reason for unexplained drops in milk production or weight gain.

Dry Matter Intake Prediction: NRC Equations and Rules

The simplest prediction is DMI as a percentage of body weight:

  • Beef cows, mid-gestation: 2.0% of BW
  • Beef cows, late gestation/lactating: 2.2–2.5% of BW
  • Growing beef cattle: 2.5–3.0% of BW
  • Dairy cows, early lactation (40+ kg milk): 3.5–4.2% of BW
  • Dairy cows, mid-lactation: 3.0–3.5% of BW
  • Sheep, maintenance: 2.5–3.0% of BW
  • Goats, lactating: 3.0–4.0% of BW

The NRC (2001) dairy equation is more precise and accounts for milk yield and forage NDF:

DMI (kg/day) = (0.372 × FCM + 0.0968 × BW^0.75) × (1 − e^(−0.192×(NDF%/100) + 0.538))

where FCM is fat-corrected milk in kg, BW is body weight in kg, and NDF% is the neutral detergent fiber content of the forage. For practical on-farm use, the percentage-of-BW method is usually sufficient, but the NRC equation is valuable when balancing rations for high-producing cows.

As-Fed to Dry Matter Conversion

Converting between as-fed (what you weigh) and DM (what you balance) is straightforward:

DM (lb) = As-fed (lb) × (DM% ÷ 100)
As-fed (lb) = DM (lb) ÷ (DM% ÷ 100)

Example: You feed 40 lb of corn silage testing 35% DM. DM delivered = 40 × 0.35 = 14 lb. If the cow needs 52 lb DM total, the remaining 38 lb DM must come from hay and grain. At 88% DM hay, that is 38 ÷ 0.88 = 43.2 lb of hay as-fed.

For nutrient concentrations, the conversion is the inverse — nutrients become more concentrated on a DM basis:

Nutrient % (DM basis) = Nutrient % (as-fed) ÷ (DM% ÷ 100)

Silage testing 8% crude protein as-fed at 35% DM = 8 ÷ 0.35 = 22.9% CP on a DM basis. This is why forage analysis reports always include both as-fed and DM columns.

Worked Example: Balancing a Dairy Cow Ration

Cow: 1,300 lb (590 kg), 90 days in milk, producing 75 lb (34 kg) milk, 3.8% fat. Predicted DMI = 3.8% of BW = 49.4 lb DM (the NRC equation gives ~51 lb; we use 50 lb for simplicity). Requirements: 32 lb net energy for lactation (NEL) at 0.72 Mcal/lb, 17% crude protein, 30% NDF.

Available feeds: Alfalfa hay (88% DM, 20% CP, 40% NDF, 0.58 Mcal NEL/lb DM); Corn silage (35% DM, 8% CP as-fed = 22.9% CP DM, 45% NDF, 0.70 Mcal/lb DM); Corn grain (88% DM, 9% CP, 10% NDF, 0.94 Mcal/lb DM); Soybean meal (89% DM, 48% CP, 0.85 Mcal/lb DM).

Ration (DM basis): 20 lb alfalfa hay + 14 lb corn silage DM (= 40 lb as-fed) + 12 lb corn grain + 4 lb soybean meal = 50 lb DM total.

Check CP: (20×0.20 + 14×0.229 + 12×0.09 + 4×0.48) ÷ 50 = (4.0 + 3.21 + 1.08 + 1.92) ÷ 50 = 10.21 ÷ 50 = 20.4% CP — above the 17% target, good.
Check NEL: (20×0.58 + 14×0.70 + 12×0.94 + 4×0.85) = 11.6 + 9.8 + 11.28 + 3.4 = 36.08 Mcal — above the 32 Mcal target, good.
Check NDF: (20×0.40 + 14×0.45 + 12×0.10 + 4×0.10) ÷ 50 = (8 + 6.3 + 1.2 + 0.4) ÷ 50 = 15.9 ÷ 50 = 31.8% — within target.

The ration balances. As-fed amounts to deliver: 22.7 lb alfalfa hay, 40 lb corn silage, 13.6 lb corn grain, 4.5 lb soybean meal — total 80.8 lb as-fed, delivering 50 lb DM.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Using as-fed nutrient percentages for balancing. Always convert to DM basis before comparing to NRC requirements. An as-fed CP of 8% in silage sounds low but is 23% on DM — adequate.

2. Assuming constant moisture. Silage moisture changes from the top to the bottom of the pit, and hay moisture varies by cutting. Test feeds regularly, especially when opening a new silage pile or hay lot.

3. Overestimating DMI in early lactation. Fresh cows often eat 10–15% less than predicted for the first 2–3 weeks. Factor this in or cows will lose excessive body condition.

4. Ignoring NDF fill limits. Cattle can only eat so much fiber. If forage NDF exceeds 35–40% of ration DM, DMI will be physically limited regardless of energy needs.

5. Not adjusting for heat stress. DMI drops 10–25% when temperature-humidity index (THI) exceeds 72. Provide shade, sprinklers, and more nutrient-dense rations in summer.

Conclusion

Dry matter intake is the bridge between what you feed and what the animal actually receives. Predict DMI as 2–4% of body weight depending on species and production stage, always convert as-fed weights to DM using tested moisture content, and balance rations on a DM basis against NRC requirements. Test your forages, recalculate when moisture changes, and monitor actual intake against predictions to catch problems early.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate dry matter intake for cattle?

The standard DMI formula for beef cattle is: DMI (% of body weight) = 3.0 - 0.012 × (mature weight - current weight) for growing cattle, or DMI = 2.0-2.5% of body weight for mature maintenance cattle. For dairy cows, DMI (kg/day) = (0.372 × FCM + 0.0968 × BW^0.75) × (1 - e^(-0.192×(WOL+3.67))). Use a dry matter intake calculator for livestock to get precise estimates based on species, weight, production level, and forage quality.

What factors affect dry matter intake?

Key factors include: body weight (larger animals eat more), production level (lactating/growing animals eat more), forage quality (higher digestibility = higher intake), feed palatability, environmental temperature (heat stress reduces intake by 10-30%), water availability, feed processing, and health status. Neutral Detergent Fiber (NDF) content is the primary chemical predictor — intake generally declines as NDF exceeds 35-40% of diet DM.

How much dry matter does a cow eat per day?

A mature beef cow (550 kg) in maintenance eats approximately 11-14 kg DM/day (2.0-2.5% BW). A lactating dairy cow (650 kg, 30 kg milk/day) eats 18-24 kg DM/day (3.0-3.5% BW). Growing feedlot cattle (400 kg) eat 10-12 kg DM/day. Sheep eat 2.5-3.5% BW in DM, and goats 3-4% BW. Always adjust for body condition, pregnancy, and environmental conditions.

Why is dry matter intake important for ration balancing?

DMI is the foundation of all livestock nutrition. Knowing DMI allows you to: calculate total nutrient requirements (energy, protein, minerals) per day, determine appropriate forage-to-concentrate ratios, avoid over- or under-feeding, optimize feed conversion efficiency, predict production levels, and manage feed costs. If DMI estimates are wrong by 10%, all nutrient supply calculations are wrong by 10%, potentially costing hundreds of dollars per head annually in wasted feed or lost production.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is dry matter intake in livestock?

Dry matter intake (DMI) is the amount of feed an animal consumes excluding all water. It is the standard basis for balancing rations because all nutrient requirements are expressed on a DM basis.

How do I calculate DMI for a cow?

A practical estimate is 2.0–4.2% of body weight depending on production stage. For a 1,300-lb dairy cow in mid-lactation, 3.2% × 1,300 = 41.6 lb DM/day. The NRC equation is more precise for high producers.

What is the difference between as-fed and dry matter?

As-fed is the feed as it comes out of the bag or pile, including moisture. Dry matter is what remains after water is removed. Nutrient requirements are always on a DM basis.

How does moisture affect how much I need to feed?

Higher moisture means less dry matter per pound. A 50% DM silage delivers half the DM of 100% DM feed at the same weight. You must feed more as-fed weight of wet feeds to meet DMI targets.

Why does DMI drop in hot weather?

Cattle reduce intake during heat stress to lower metabolic heat production. DMI can drop 10–25% when THI exceeds 72. Provide cooling and increase ration nutrient density to compensate.
