Soil Testing & Parameters

Bulk Density: The Simple Test That Reveals Soil Compaction

14 Sep 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 5 min read

Related Calculators: bulk density calculator

Compaction rarely announces itself. Crops simply underperform on part of the field while the soil looks normal from the cab. A bulk density test — one metal ring, an oven and a scale — turns that vague suspicion into a number you can compare against thresholds and track over the years.

What Bulk Density Measures and Why It Matters

Bulk density is the oven-dry weight of soil divided by the volume it occupied in the field, expressed in grams per cubic centimeter. It captures the balance between solid particles and pore space: the same loam can hold half its volume in pores when mellow, or far less when wheel traffic has crushed those pores shut.

Roots, water and air all travel through pores, so rising bulk density means shrinking room for all three. Soil health assessment frameworks used by USDA NRCS treat bulk density as a core physical indicator precisely because it integrates compaction, aggregation and organic matter decline into one measurable figure.

Reference values to judge against

Typical undisturbed values run about 1.10 to 1.30 g/cm3 for clays and clay loams, 1.20 to 1.40 for loams and silt loams, and 1.40 to 1.55 for sandy soils — sands tolerate higher numbers because their particles are larger. General guide values for restricted rooting start near 1.55 to 1.75 on sands, 1.40 to 1.55 on loams and 1.30 to 1.40 on clays; the finer the soil, the lower the density at which roots begin to struggle.

The Core Method: Field Steps and Calculation

Step 1: take the core

Smooth the surface, press a standard ring sampler (commonly 5 cm tall by 5 cm inner diameter) straight into the top 5 or 15 cm of soil, then dig it out and trim both ends flush with a knife so the soil exactly fills the known volume. A 5 × 5 cm ring holds 98.2 cm3 (3.1416 × 2.5² × 5).

Step 2: dry and weigh

Bag the ring's contents, dry to constant weight at 105°C — overnight in an oven, or several days in the sun for field approximations — and weigh the dry soil without the ring. Moisture must be gone, or the number is inflated.

Step 3: the formulas

Bulk density (g/cm3) = oven-dry soil weight ÷ ring volume. Porosity (%) = (1 − bulk density ÷ 2.65) × 100, using 2.65 g/cm3 as the typical particle density of mineral soil. Percent compaction versus a reference = (field density ÷ reference density − 1) × 100, where the reference is the same soil type under a fence line or headland that never sees traffic.

Worked Example: Wheel-Track Versus Middle of the Bed

On a loam field you take two cores: one in the wheel track of the sprayer, one mid-bed. The wheel-track sample weighs 149 g dry at 98.2 cm3: 149 ÷ 98.2 = 1.52 g/cm3. The mid-bed sample weighs 132 g: 132 ÷ 98.2 = 1.34 g/cm3. The untouched fence-line reference runs 1.25 g/cm3.

Porosity in the wheel track is (1 − 1.52 ÷ 2.65) × 100 = 42.6%, versus 49.4% mid-bed and 52.8% on the reference. Compaction versus reference: 1.52 ÷ 1.25 − 1 = 21.6% in the track, 7.2% in the bed. Interpretation: the loam guide threshold of about 1.40 is already crossed in the wheel track — expect slower infiltration and stunted roots there — while the bed remains workable. Controlled traffic or deeper ripping ahead of planting targets exactly the track zone.

How many cores per zone?

Three to five cores per zone is the practical minimum; average them before judging. Take companion samples for gravimetric moisture if you want to convert to a common moisture basis for year-to-year comparison.

Mistakes and Practical Notes

Sampling Too Wet or Too Dry

Pressing rings into mud smears the walls and seals pores; bone-dry soil shatters instead of cores. Aim for workable moisture — the ball that crumbles when poked — the same condition used for other field tests.

Reading One Number as a Verdict

A single high core can sit over an old stump hole or a burrow. Bulk density varies naturally with texture, so always compare against the same soil type's reference, never against a neighbor's field or a book value for a different texture.

Forgetting Stones

Stony soils complicate the ring method because stones occupy volume without contributing pore function. Where stones are frequent, note them, exclude obviously stone-filled cores or switch to the clod method used in research settings.

Repeating the same zones each season turns the test into a trend line: numbers falling after cover crops or reduced tillage are the cheapest proof that a soil-building program is working. Frequently Asked Questions

What bulk density is bad for my soil?

It depends on texture: general restriction thresholds begin near 1.55 to 1.75 g/cm3 on sands, 1.40 to 1.55 on loams and 1.30 to 1.40 on clays. Always compare against a reference sample of your own soil type rather than the guide alone.

How do I measure bulk density without a ring sampler?

You can drive an open tin can of known volume into the soil, or excavate a small hole, weigh all removed dry soil and fill the hole with measured sand to get its volume. The ring method is simply the most repeatable.

Why did my bulk density increase after tillage?

Tillage temporarily loosens the surface but destroys aggregates and compresses the layer beneath; over seasons the re-compacted structure often tests denser than before. Long-term comparisons matter more than the number right after a pass.

Does bulk density affect water holding?

Yes. Pore space holds plant-available water, so every point of density gained is pore volume lost. That is why compacted zones both drain slower when wet and store less water for the crop when dry.
