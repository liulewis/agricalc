Irrigation Water Calculation: From ET to Run Time

Run Time Formula: Run time (hours) = (Net requirement (inches) × Area (acres) × 27,154) ÷ (System flow rate (gpm) × Application efficiency × 60)

11 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 15 min read

The Irrigation Water Calculation Framework: ET, Crop Coefficients, and Run Time

Irrigation scheduling answers two questions: when to irrigate and how much water to apply. The FAO-56 methodology (Allen et al., 1998) is the global standard used by agricultural engineers, irrigation districts, and farm advisors worldwide. The framework has five steps: (1) determine reference evapotranspiration (ET0), (2) select crop coefficients (Kc) by growth stage, (3) calculate crop evapotranspiration (ETc = ET0 × Kc), (4) subtract effective rainfall and soil water contributions to get net irrigation requirement, (5) convert to system run time.

Understanding this framework turns irrigation from guesswork into a precise, repeatable process. Over-irrigation wastes water, leaches nutrients, and promotes root diseases; under-irrigation stresses the crop and caps yield.

Step 1: Reference Evapotranspiration (ET0)

ET0 is the water use of a standardized reference crop (well-watered grass, 8–15 cm tall, actively growing, completely shading the ground). It represents the atmospheric evaporative demand independent of crop type and soil water. ET0 is calculated from weather data using the Penman-Monteith equation (FAO-56 standard).

Typical ET0 ranges: 0.10–0.20 in/day in spring, 0.25–0.35 in/day in mid-summer, 0.05–0.15 in/day in fall.

Step 2: Crop Coefficients (Kc)

The crop coefficient (Kc) adjusts ET0 to the specific crop and its growth stage. Kc varies throughout the season based on crop height, canopy cover, leaf area, and stomatal resistance. Each crop has a characteristic Kc curve with four stages.

Kc values from FAO-56 (Allen et al., 1998) and university extension. Adjust Kc for local conditions: add 0.05–0.10 for windy, low-humidity climates; subtract 0.05–0.10 for humid, calm climates. For drip irrigation, Kc may be 0.10–0.20 lower because only part of the soil surface is wetted (reduced soil evaporation).

Step 3: Crop Evapotranspiration (ETc)

ETc is the actual water use of the crop under well-watered conditions (no water stress). It is expressed in inches/day or mm/day. Multiply by the number of days in an irrigation interval to get total crop water use for that period.

Example: Corn at mid-season (Kc = 1.18), ET0 = 0.30 in/day. ETc = 0.30 × 1.18 = 0.354 in/day. Over a 7-day interval: 0.354 × 7 = 2.48 inches of crop water use.

Step 4: Net Irrigation Requirement Calculation for Crop Water Demand

Not all crop water use needs to come from irrigation. Effective rainfall and stored soil water contribute to crop water supply.

Effective rainfall: The portion of total rainfall that enters the root zone and is available to the crop. Not all rain is effective — light showers (<0.1 in) may evaporate before infiltrating; heavy rain may run off or drain below the root zone. A common approximation: effective rainfall = total rainfall × 0.70–0.85 (lower for intense storms, higher for gentle, soaking rains). USDA NRCS provides more detailed methods (SCS method).

Soil water contribution: The plant-available water (PAW) stored in the root zone at the start of the irrigation interval. PAW = (field capacity − permanent wilting point) × root zone depth × bulk density. Typical PAW: sandy soils 0.5–1.0 in/ft, loams 1.5–2.0 in/ft, clays 1.2–1.8 in/ft. Manage allowable depletion (MAD): typically 40–60% of PAW for most crops (50% is common). Irrigate when soil water depletion reaches MAD.

Leaching requirement: Needed only on saline soils or when using saline irrigation water. LR = ECiw ÷ (5 × ECdw − ECiw), where ECiw = irrigation water salinity (dS/m), ECdw = maximum allowable soil salinity for the crop. Most non-saline situations: LR = 0.

Irrigation Scheduling Strategies

Calendar-based: Irrigate on a fixed schedule (e.g., every 7 days). Simple but does not account for weather variability. Risk of over- or under-irrigation.

Soil moisture-based: Use soil moisture sensors (tensiometers, capacitance sensors, neutron probe) to trigger irrigation when soil water reaches the management allowable depletion (MAD) threshold. Most accurate method. Sensors cost $200–1,000 per station.

ET-based/Checkbook method: Track daily ETc and rainfall, maintain a soil water "balance" (like a checkbook). Irrigate when the balance reaches the MAD threshold. Can be done with a spreadsheet or app (e.g., CropManage, IrrigAZ).

Plant stress-based: Use infrared thermometers (canopy temperature), pressure chambers (stem water potential), or visual symptoms. Reactive rather than proactive — by the time stress is visible, yield may already be affected. Best used as a complement to soil moisture or ET scheduling.

Worked Example: Center Pivot Irrigation for Corn, 125 acres

A quarter-mile center pivot (125 acres, radius 1,320 ft) in central Kansas, irrigating corn in mid-July. System flow rate: 750 gpm. Application efficiency: 85% (typical for center pivot in moderate wind). Soil: silt loam, plant-available water = 1.8 in/ft, root zone = 4 ft, total PAW = 7.2 inches. Management allowable depletion (MAD) = 50%.

Step 1 — ET0 and Kc: Mid-July in Kansas: average ET0 = 0.30 in/day (from local weather station). Corn at tasseling/silking (mid-season): Kc = 1.20.

Step 2 — Crop water use (ETc): ETc = 0.30 × 1.20 = 0.36 in/day. Over 7 days: 0.36 × 7 = 2.52 inches.

Step 3 — Effective rainfall: Rainfall in the past 7 days: 0.6 inches (two gentle showers). Effective rainfall = 0.6 × 0.80 = 0.48 inches.

Step 4 — Soil water check: Soil moisture sensor at 2 ft depth reads 55% of PAW depleted. Irrigation trigger (MAD = 50%) has been reached. Available soil water remaining = 7.2 × 0.45 = 3.24 inches, but we want to refill to field capacity, so apply the amount depleted: 7.2 × 0.55 = 3.96 inches. However, we only need to replace ETc minus rain since last irrigation: net = 2.52 − 0.48 = 2.04 inches. Apply 2.04 inches (slightly less than full depletion to avoid over-watering).

Step 5 — Calculate run time: Run time = (2.04 inches × 125 acres × 27,154 gal/acre-in) ÷ (750 gpm × 0.85 × 60 min/hr) = (2.04 × 125 × 27,154) ÷ (38,250) = 6,920,100 ÷ 38,250 = 180.9 hours. Wait — that is for the full 125 acres. A center pivot makes a full circle in this time. 180.9 hours ≈ 7.5 days for one full revolution. This means the pivot can just keep up with crop water demand at this time of year. If ET increases to 0.35 in/day, the pivot will not be able to keep up (run time > irrigation interval), and yield loss will occur. This is a critical planning check.

Step 6 — Verify system capacity: System capacity = (750 gpm × 0.85 × 1440 min/day) ÷ (125 acres × 27,154 gal/acre-in) = (918,000) ÷ (3,394,250) = 0.270 in/day. This is the maximum water the system can apply per day. Crop ETc = 0.36 in/day. The system is undersized for peak demand! Options: (1) reduce irrigated area, (2) increase flow rate (larger pump/well), (3) accept deficit irrigation and reduced yield, (4) use soil water storage from pre-season irrigation to buffer peak demand. This calculation reveals a serious limitation that calendar-based scheduling would miss.

Common Irrigation Calculation Mistakes

  • Using ET0 directly as crop water need (forgetting Kc): Always multiply ET0 by the crop coefficient for the current growth stage. A crop with Kc = 0.3 (initial stage) uses only 30% of ET0; at mid-season Kc = 1.2, it uses 120% of ET0. Using ET0 directly over-irrigates early and under-irrigates at peak demand.
  • Ignoring effective rainfall: Subtract effective rainfall from ETc before calculating irrigation need. A 0.5-inch rain can provide 1–2 days of crop water use. Failing to account for rainfall leads to over-irrigation, nutrient leaching, and wasted water/pumping cost.
  • Using 100% application efficiency: Every irrigation system loses water: evaporation (sprinkler), drift (wind), deep percolation, runoff. Use realistic efficiency: drip 90%, center pivot 85%, furrow 60%. Applying the net requirement at 100% efficiency under-applies by 10–40%.
  • Not checking system capacity against peak ET: Calculate your system's maximum daily application (in/day) and compare to peak crop ETc. If system capacity < peak ETc, the system cannot keep up during high-demand periods, and yield loss is inevitable. This is the most important pre-season planning calculation.
  • Irrigating on a fixed calendar schedule regardless of weather: Use soil moisture sensors or ET-based scheduling to adjust irrigation timing and amount. A week of cool, cloudy weather can reduce ETc by 50%, making a scheduled irrigation unnecessary. Fixed schedules waste 20–30% of irrigation water on average.
  • Forgetting to account for irrigation system start-up losses: When starting an irrigation system after a dry period, the first 10–30 minutes may be lost to filling pipelines, wetting dry soil cracks, and evaporating from hot surfaces. Add 5–10% to calculated run time for the first irrigation after a long dry spell.

Practical Case Study: Real-World Application

Consider a 50-hectare mixed farm in the Midwest United States implementing this topic. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) guidelines on sustainable agricultural practices, systematic implementation of evidence-based techniques can improve resource use efficiency by 15–25% while maintaining or increasing yields. The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) recommends starting with a 10% adoption rate in the first season, scaling up as results are verified.

In practice, this means conducting a baseline assessment during the off-season, identifying the top two priority areas for improvement, and allocating a small trial area (2–5% of total acreage) to test new practices before full-scale implementation. Documenting inputs, costs, and outcomes on the trial plot provides actionable data for future decisions.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Irrigation water calculation follows a clear, five-step process: ET0 → Kc → ETc → net requirement → run time. The FAO-56 methodology provides the standard framework, and the formulas are straightforward once you have weather data, crop coefficients, and system specifications. FAQ

Where do I get ET0 data for my location?

ET0 data is available from: (1) State agricultural weather networks (CIMIS in CA, CoAgMet in CO, AZMET in AZ, Georgia Weather, etc.), (2) National Weather Service / NOAA stations, (3) Online calculators like the FAO ET0 calculator or Penman-Monteith tools, (4) Commercial weather services (DTN, Iteris, Bushnell). Many of these provide daily ET0 values free of charge. For the most accurate results, use a station within 10–20 miles of your field with similar elevation and terrain.

How often should I recalculate irrigation need?

Daily or every 2–3 days during peak water use (mid-summer). Weekly is sufficient in spring and fall when ET is low. The checkbook method (tracking daily ETc and rainfall) is the most practical approach — update the soil water balance daily and irrigate when it reaches the MAD threshold. Soil moisture sensors provide real-time data and eliminate the need for manual calculation.

What is the difference between gross and net irrigation requirement?

Net irrigation requirement is the amount of water that must reach the crop root zone (ETc − effective rainfall − soil water). Gross irrigation requirement is the amount of water that must be pumped/applied, accounting for system losses: Gross = Net ÷ application efficiency. For example, if net need = 2.0 inches and efficiency = 85%, gross = 2.0 ÷ 0.85 = 2.35 inches. The difference (0.35 inches) is lost to evaporation, drift, deep percolation, etc.

Can I use the same Kc values for all varieties of a crop?

Generally yes — Kc values are crop-specific, not variety-specific, because they depend primarily on canopy development, height, and ground cover, which are similar across varieties of the same crop. However, there are exceptions: (1) indeterminate vs. determinate varieties may have different canopy duration, (2) very early-maturing varieties reach mid-season Kc faster but have a shorter mid-season period, (3) dwarf/semi-dwarf varieties may have slightly lower Kc due to shorter height. Adjust Kc by ±0.05–0.10 if your variety differs significantly from the standard.

How do I measure my irrigation system flow rate?

The most accurate method is a permanent or portable flow meter installed on the main pipeline (cost: $200–2,000). If no meter is available: (1) For pump systems, use the pump performance curve from the manufacturer and measure total dynamic head (TDH), (2) For center pivots, measure the time for one full revolution and calculate from the system's rated flow, (3) Use a bucket and stopwatch for small drip systems (fill a 5-gallon bucket, time it, calculate gpm), (4) Use an ultrasonic flow meter (clamp-on, no pipe cutting needed, cost: $500–3,000). Always verify flow rate — a 20% error in flow rate causes a 20% error in run time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate crop water requirement for irrigation?

Crop water requirement (ETc) = Reference evapotranspiration (ETo) × Crop coefficient (Kc). ETo comes from local weather data (Penman-Monteith method) or a nearby weather station. Kc varies by crop growth stage: 0.3–0.5 for initial stage, 0.7–1.0 for mid-season, 0.6–0.8 for late season. For example, tomatoes in mid-season with ETo = 0.25 in/day and Kc = 1.0 need 0.25 inches/day = 1.75 inches/week. A crop water requirement calculator simplifies this for any crop and stage.

How do I calculate irrigation run time?

Irrigation run time (hours) = Water needed (inches) × Area (acres) × 27,154 ÷ (Flow rate (GPM) × 60 × Application efficiency). For drip irrigation (90% efficiency) applying 1 inch to 1 acre at 50 GPM: (1 × 1 × 27,154) ÷ (50 × 60 × 0.90) = 27,154 ÷ 2,700 = 10.06 hours. An irrigation run time calculator accounts for system type, efficiency, and area automatically.

What is evapotranspiration and why does it matter for irrigation?

Evapotranspiration (ET) is the combined loss of water from soil evaporation and plant transpiration. It represents the amount of water that must be replaced by irrigation (plus rainfall). Reference ET (ETo) is measured for a standard grass surface and adjusted by crop coefficient (Kc) to get actual crop ET (ETc). Understanding ET allows precise irrigation scheduling that avoids both underwatering (yield loss) and overwatering (waste, disease, leaching). Use an evapotranspiration calculator with local weather data.

How do I calculate irrigation flow rate for my system?

Flow rate depends on water source and system design. For a well: GPM = Well yield (tested). For municipal supply: GPM = Pressure (PSI) × Pipe factor. For a designed system: Total GPM = Number of emitters × Flow per emitter ÷ 120 (for two zones). For example, 1,000 drip emitters at 0.5 GPH each = 500 GPH = 8.33 GPM per zone. An irrigation flow rate calculator helps match pump size, pipe diameter, and zone layout to avoid pressure loss.

How much water does an acre of vegetables need per week?

Water needs vary by crop, stage, climate, and soil. Typical peak-season requirements: tomatoes 1.5–2.5 inches/week (4,000–6,800 gallons/acre/day), lettuce 1.0–1.5 inches/week, peppers 1.0–2.0 inches/week, cucumbers 1.5–2.5 inches/week. One inch of water on one acre = 27,154 gallons. In hot, dry climates, demand can exceed 3 inches/week. Use a crop water requirement calculator with local ETo data for precise scheduling.

What is irrigation efficiency and how is it calculated?

Irrigation efficiency is the percentage of applied water that reaches the crop root zone (not lost to evaporation, runoff, or deep percolation). Typical efficiencies: drip irrigation 85–95%, sprinkler 70–85%, flood/furrow 50–70%. Efficiency = (Water stored in root zone ÷ Water applied) × 100. To calculate required application: Water to apply = Crop water need ÷ Efficiency. For example, if crop needs 1 inch and drip efficiency is 90%, apply 1 ÷ 0.90 = 1.11 inches. Higher efficiency reduces water and pumping costs.
