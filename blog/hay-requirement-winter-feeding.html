Winter Hay Feeding: How Much Hay Does a Cow Need?

3 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 7 min read

Introduction

Winter hay feeding is one of the largest annual costs for cow-calf operations in temperate and continental climates. A 1,200-pound pregnant beef cow can consume 25 to 35 pounds of hay per day, and over a 150-day feeding period that translates to roughly 2 to 3 tons per head. Getting the estimate wrong means either running out of feed in February — when prices are highest — or tying up capital in surplus hay that spoils before it can be used.

This guide walks through the actual formulas extension livestock specialists use: dry matter intake as a percentage of body weight, conversion from dry matter to as-fed basis, waste allowances for different feeding methods, and total bale counts. We include a worked example for a 40-head herd and highlight the most common miscalculations that cost producers thousands of dollars each winter.

Hay Requirement Calculator: Daily Cow Intake

The starting point is dry matter intake (DMI), expressed as a percentage of the animal's body weight. A mature beef cow in mid-gestation consumes approximately 2.0% of her body weight in dry matter per day. In late gestation (third trimester) that rises to 2.2–2.5%, and for a lactating cow in early lactation it can reach 2.5–3.0%. These figures come from the NRC (National Research Council) nutrient requirements of beef cattle and are widely used by university extension services.

To convert dry matter to as-fed hay — what you actually pull off the truck — divide by the dry matter percentage of the forage. Good grass hay typically tests 85–90% DM; alfalfa 88–92%; wet wrapped baleage may be only 50–60% DM. The formula is:

As-fed intake (lb/day) = (Body weight × DMI%) ÷ Hay DM%

For a 1,200-lb cow at 2.2% DMI eating 88% DM grass hay: (1,200 × 0.022) ÷ 0.88 = 30 lb/day as-fed. This is the number most producers actually need — it is what you will feed out of the bale.

Winter Hay Feeding: Total Requirement and Waste

Multiply daily intake by the number of feeding days. The feeding period varies: 120 days in the southern Great Plains, 150–180 days in the Upper Midwest and Canada, up to 210 days in parts of the Mountain West. Using 150 days as a baseline, our 1,200-lb cow needs 30 × 150 = 4,500 lb, or 2.25 tons, of hay.

But this assumes zero waste, which never happens. Waste factors depend heavily on feeding method:

  • Unrolling on the ground: 25–35% waste (trampling, fouling, refusal)
  • Open bale feeder (cone or ring): 10–15% waste
  • Hay rack with trailer or covered feeder: 5–10% waste
  • Processing/grinding into a bunk: 3–8% waste

Applying a conservative 15% waste for ring feeders: 4,500 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = 5,294 lb, or about 2.65 tons per cow. For a 40-head herd that is 106 tons. With 1,200-lb round bales, you need 106 × 2,000 ÷ 1,200 ≈ 177 bales. Many producers add a further 10% contingency for a late spring or poor first-cutting quality, bringing the practical target to roughly 195 bales.

Worked Example: 40-Head Herd, 165-Day Winter

Let us run a complete calculation for a representative operation. Herd: 40 mature Angus-cross cows averaging 1,250 lb, all in late gestation by January. Feeding period: November 15 to April 30 = 167 days, rounded to 165. Hay: mixed grass-legume, 88% DM, 1,100-lb round bales. Feeding method: cone-style ring feeders, estimated 12% waste.

Step 1 — Daily DMI: 1,250 × 0.023 (late gestation) = 28.75 lb DM/day.
Step 2 — As-fed: 28.75 ÷ 0.88 = 32.7 lb/day.
Step 3 — Total per cow: 32.7 × 165 = 5,396 lb.
Step 4 — Add waste: 5,396 ÷ 0.88 = 6,132 lb = 3.07 tons/cow.
Step 5 — Herd total: 6,132 × 40 = 245,280 lb = 122.6 tons.
Step 6 — Bales: 245,280 ÷ 1,100 = 223 bales. Add 10% contingency → 245 bales.

At a delivered price of $85 per bale, the total feed budget is $20,825. Underestimating waste by just 10 percentage points would leave you short about 25 bales — a $2,125 emergency purchase at February spot prices, which often run 20–30% higher than summer contracts.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Ignoring hay quality and moisture. A 50% DM baleage bale weighs the same as an 88% DM hay bale on the scale, but delivers only 57% of the dry matter. Always test or ask for a forage analysis; moisture content is the single biggest source of error in hay budgeting.

2. Forgetting the cow's changing requirements. A cow in early lactation eats 30–40% more than one in mid-gestation. If your calving season falls in mid-winter, your January and February intake will be far higher than your November average. Weight the feeding period by stage of production.

3. Underestimating waste. Ground-feeding without a feeder can waste a third of every bale. Investing $300–$600 in a good ring feeder typically pays for itself in a single winter through reduced waste.

4. Not accounting for storage loss. Hay stored outside uncovered loses 15–25% of dry matter in the outer layer over six months. Elevate bales on pallets or gravel, cover with tarps, or budget for the shrink.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Accurate winter hay budgeting comes down to four numbers: body weight × intake percentage ÷ dry matter × feeding days, then adjusted for waste and contingency. For most cow-calf operations, plan on 2.5 to 3.0 tons of hay per 1,200-lb cow for a 150-day winter, and budget 200–250 round bales per 40-head herd. Test your hay, use a feeder, and store it properly — these three practices alone can cut your winter feed bill by 15–20%.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many bales of hay does a cow need for winter?

A 1,200-lb beef cow typically needs 2.5–3.0 tons of hay for a 150-day winter. With 1,200-lb round bales that is roughly 4–5 bales per cow, before adding a 10% contingency.

What percentage of body weight do cows eat in hay?

Mature beef cows consume 2.0–2.5% of body weight in dry matter per day. Late-gestation and lactating cows are at the higher end; mid-gestation cows at the lower end.

How much hay is wasted when feeding on the ground?

Ground feeding without a feeder typically wastes 25–35% of the hay through trampling, fouling, and refusal. A ring feeder reduces this to 10–15%.

Does hay moisture content affect how much I need to buy?

Yes. Hay at 50% DM (wet baleage) delivers only 57% of the dry matter of 88% DM hay at the same scale weight. Always convert to a dry-matter basis before budgeting.

How long should I plan to feed hay in winter?

Feeding periods range from 120 days (southern Great Plains) to 180+ days (northern tier, Mountain West). Use your average first-frost to last-grazing date and add a 2-week buffer.
