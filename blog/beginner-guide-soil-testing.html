A Beginner's Guide to Soil Testing: Why, When, and How

1 Sep 2026 AT 4 min read

Soil testing is one of the most valuable investments a farmer can make. Yet many smallholder farmers never test their soil, relying instead on tradition or guesswork for fertilization decisions. This guide covers everything you need to know to get started with soil testing.

Why Test Your Soil?

Soil testing provides critical information about your soil's nutrient status, pH level, organic matter content, and texture. Without this information, you are essentially fertilizing blind — either over-applying (wasting money and polluting water) or under-applying (limiting yields). A basic soil test costs $10-30 per sample and can save hundreds of dollars in unnecessary fertilizer while increasing yields.

When to Test

The best time to test soil is in the fall after harvest, or in early spring before planting. Testing in fall gives you time to plan fertilization and apply lime if needed (lime takes 3-6 months to react). Avoid testing immediately after fertilizer or manure application, as this will give artificially high nutrient readings. Test every 2-3 years for established fields, and annually for high-value crops or fields with known deficiencies.

How to Collect a Proper Sample

Sample collection is the most important step — a poorly collected sample gives misleading results. Follow these steps:

  1. Divide your field into sampling zones based on soil type, slope, previous management, and crop performance (problem areas separately).
  2. For each zone, collect 15-20 subsamples in a zigzag pattern across the area.
  3. Use a soil probe or auger to sample from 0-20cm depth (topsoil) for most crops. For deeper-rooted crops, also sample 20-60cm.
  4. Mix all subsamples in a clean bucket, remove rocks and plant debris, and take 500g (1 pint) as the composite sample.
  5. Place in a labeled bag or box with your name, field ID, date, and crop plans.

Understanding Your Results

A standard soil test report includes: pH, buffer pH (for lime requirement), organic matter, cation exchange capacity (CEC), base saturation (Ca, Mg, K), and available nutrients (P, K, Ca, Mg, S, and micronutrients). Most reports also include fertilizer recommendations. Key numbers to focus on:

  • pH: 6.0-7.0 for most crops. Below 5.5 needs lime; above 7.5 may need sulfur or acidifying amendments.
  • Organic Matter: 3-5% is ideal. Below 2% indicates degraded soil; above 6% is excellent.
  • Phosphorus (P): 15-30 ppm (Bray-1) is adequate. Below 10 ppm is deficient; above 50 ppm is excessive (environmental risk).
  • Potassium (K): 100-200 ppm is adequate. Below 80 ppm is deficient.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I collect a soil sample for testing?

Divide your field into uniform zones based on soil type, slope, and past management. For each zone, collect 15–20 subsamples from the top 15–20 cm using a soil probe or augur, walking in a zigzag pattern. Mix thoroughly in a clean bucket, remove 500 g, and air-dry before sending to the lab.

How often should I test my soil?

Test every 2–3 years for established fields. For high-value vegetable crops or when making major management changes, test annually. Always test before planting a new crop or applying lime, and retest 6 months after a significant amendment application.

What does a soil test report include?

A complete agricultural soil test includes: pH, buffer pH (for lime requirement), organic matter percentage, cation exchange capacity (CEC), base saturation percentages, and extractable levels of phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur, and micronutrients (zinc, boron, manganese, iron, copper).

How do I convert soil test results to fertilizer amounts?

First determine your crop's nutrient requirement based on yield goal. Subtract the soil's available nutrient supply (from test results using local calibration curves). The difference is the fertilizer needed. Use a fertilizer calculator to convert nutrient requirements (kg N/ha) into actual product amounts (kg urea/ha) based on fertilizer grade.
