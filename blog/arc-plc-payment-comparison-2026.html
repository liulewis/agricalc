ARC vs PLC 2026: How to Choose the Right Farm Safety Net

5 Oct 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 8 min read

Related Calculators: ARC/PLC payment calculator · crop insurance calculator · break-even price calculator

Introduction

For U.S. growers of covered commodities, the annual ARC/PLC election is one of the highest-stakes decisions on the farm business calendar. The 2026 crop year changes the math in two important ways. First, the program election is annual again starting in 2026, so last year's choice no longer locks you in — every crop year is a fresh decision. Second, new legislation (OBBBA) has raised statutory reference prices for major crops: corn moves from $3.70 to $4.10 per bushel, soybeans from $8.40 to $10.00, and wheat from $5.50 to $6.35.

Higher reference prices make Price Loss Coverage (PLC) more competitive in low-price years, while Agriculture Risk Coverage (ARC) still shines when revenue — not just price — takes the hit. Choosing well can mean thousands of dollars per farm, and choosing on autopilot can leave money on the table. This guide walks through both payment formulas step by step, shows exactly how a 2026 payment is calculated on a real corn example, and flags the mistakes that cost producers money. Key Principles and Concepts

Price Loss Coverage (PLC) protects against low prices. It starts from the effective reference price, which equals the 5-year Olympic average of marketing-year-average (MYA) prices multiplied by 88%, capped at 115% of the statutory reference price. A PLC payment is triggered when the MYA price (or the loan rate, whichever is higher) falls below that effective reference price. The per-acre payment formula is: max(effective reference price − max(MYA price, loan rate), 0) × PLC yield.

Agriculture Risk Coverage at the county level (ARC-CO) protects against low revenue, not just low prices. Its benchmark is the 5-year Olympic average county yield multiplied by the 5-year Olympic average national MYA price. The guarantee is set at 90% of that benchmark revenue. If actual county revenue falls below the guarantee, ARC pays the shortfall — capped at 12% of benchmark revenue per acre.

Two rules apply to both programs. First, the per-acre payment is multiplied by 85% of base acres — payments are made on base acres, not planted acres. Second, payments are subject to a $155,000 per-person payment limit (about $164,000 in 2026). Because the election is annual again from 2026, you can — and should — re-run this comparison every single year. Practical Implementation Strategies

The ARC/PLC election is made separately for each covered commodity on each FSA farm number, so the right answer for your corn base may differ from your soybean base. Start by gathering three things: your PLC yields and base acres by crop from your FSA records, recent county yields for ARC-CO benchmarking, and a realistic range of MYA price expectations for the coming marketing year.

Next, model both programs under at least two price scenarios — a base case near current expectations and a stress case 10–15% lower. Because ARC-CO pays on county revenue, also consider your county's yield variability: counties with volatile yields favor ARC, while stable-yield counties with price risk favor PLC. Finally, complete your election at your local FSA office during the enrollment period, and keep copies of everything. For a deeper look at whole-farm risk, see our farm financial management guide.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

The most expensive mistake is electing the same program for every crop out of habit. Corn, soybeans, and wheat have different reference prices, different yield volatility, and different price outlooks — each deserves its own calculation. A second common error is comparing programs using futures prices instead of the marketing-year-average price; PLC and ARC settle on MYA prices, which can differ substantially from the futures curve.

Other frequent errors include forgetting the 85%-of-base-acres factor (which shrinks every per-acre payment), ignoring the payment limit on large operations, and assuming ARC pays on planted acres. And with annual elections restored in 2026, the old habit of "set it and forget it" is now a liability: a choice that was right in 2024 may be wrong in 2026 after the OBBBA reference price increases.

Practical Case Study: 500 Corn Base Acres in 2026

Consider a farm with 500 corn base acres. We will use clearly marked illustrative assumptions: a PLC yield of 175 bushels per acre, an effective reference price of $4.05 per bushel (below the new $4.10 statutory price), and a marketing-year-average price of $3.80.

PLC calculation: payment rate = max($4.05 − max($3.80, loan rate), 0) = $0.25 per bushel. Per-acre payment = $0.25 × 175 bu = $43.75. Total payment = $43.75 × 85% × 500 acres = $18,593.75.

ARC-CO calculation: assume a benchmark yield of 170 bushels at the $4.05 Olympic-average price, giving benchmark revenue of $688.50 per acre. The 90% guarantee equals $619.65. If actual county revenue comes in at 160 bushels × $3.60 = $576.00, the shortfall is $43.65 per acre — below the 12% cap ($82.62) — so the payment is $43.65 per acre. Total = $43.65 × 85% × 500 = $18,551.25.

In this falling-price scenario, PLC edges out ARC by a small margin. Flip the scenario — stable prices but a county yield disaster — and ARC wins decisively. Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension farm-management economists consistently recommend the same discipline: run both programs with your own county yields and PLC yields every year, under at least two price scenarios, before you sign anything. The OBBBA reference price increases tilt the long-run odds toward PLC for corn, soybeans, and wheat — but ARC-CO remains the better tool when your county's yield risk dominates your price risk.

Practical next steps: (1) pull your base acres and PLC yields from FSA records; (2) download your county's historical yields; (3) model both programs in our ARC/PLC payment calculator; (4) cross-check against your crop insurance coverage so you are not double-counting the same risk layer; and (5) file your election at the FSA office before the enrollment deadline. A quick budget review with our farm budget calculator will show how the expected payment fits your cash flow.

Conclusion

The 2026 ARC/PLC decision is genuinely new: annual elections are back, and higher OBBBA reference prices have rewritten the PLC side of the ledger. PLC now triggers sooner and pays more in low-price years, while ARC-CO continues to protect against the revenue collapses that prices alone don't capture. There is no universal winner — the right choice varies by crop, county, and year.

Work the formulas with your own numbers, respect the 85% base-acre factor and the payment limit, and re-elect deliberately every year. For more practical tools to support your farm business decisions, explore our collection of free agricultural calculators.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I choose ARC for one crop and PLC for another?

Yes. The election is made separately for each covered commodity on each FSA farm number, so corn base can be in PLC while soybean base on the same farm is in ARC-CO — or vice versa. Model each crop independently.

Are ARC/PLC payments made on planted acres or base acres?

Base acres. The per-acre payment rate is multiplied by 85% of the farm’s base acres for the enrolled commodity, regardless of what you actually plant that year.

What is the ARC/PLC payment limit for 2026?

Payments are subject to a $155,000 per-person payment limit (about $164,000 in 2026). Large operations should check whether the limit binds before counting on full modeled payments.

Do I have to make a new ARC/PLC election every year now?

Yes. Starting with the 2026 crop year, the ARC/PLC election is annual again. Re-run the comparison each year, because the OBBBA reference price increases and changing market conditions can flip which program pays more.
