How to Use This Calculator

Measure your windrow dimensions (length, base width, peak height), enter the target application rate in t/ha, compost density (600 kg/m³ is typical for finished compost) and field area. Click Calculate.

About This Tool

Windrow volume is computed as a triangular prism: cross-section area = 0.5 × base width × height; volume = length × cross-section. Weight = volume × bulk density. Finished compost typically weighs 500–700 kg/m³; fresh raw material is lighter at 300–400 kg/m³.

Applicability: Applicable globally, with particular relevance for North American and European cropping systems. Adapt rates to your soil test results.

How the Calculation Works

Volume = L × (0.5 × W × H). Weight (t) = Volume × Density (kg/m³) ÷ 1000. Required compost (t) = target rate (t/ha) × field area (ha). Area covered = pile weight ÷ target rate.

Real-World Example

L = 20 m, W = 2 m, H = 1.5 m. Cross-section = 0.5 × 2 × 1.5 = 1.5 m². Volume = 20 × 1.5 = 30 m³. Weight = 30 × 600 ÷ 1000 = 18 t. At 10 t/ha, covers 1.8 ha.

Common Mistakes & Tips

Common mistakes: using a rectangular cross-section (overestimates volume), forgetting that finished compost loses 30–50% of its volume during curing, over-applying compost (P builds up rapidly), and not turning piles often enough. Thermophilic piles should be turned every 5–7 days to maintain 55–65 °C.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I turn a compost pile?

For thermophilic (hot) composting, turn every 5–7 days to keep temperatures at 55–65 °C and ensure even decomposition. In slow or cold composting, turn every 3–4 weeks. Use a thermometer: if the pile drops below 40 °C it is not actively composting.

How do I size a compost pile?

Aerobic windrows should be at least 1 m wide × 1 m high (to heat up) but no wider than 2.5 m or higher than 1.5 m (to allow oxygen penetration). This calculator uses a triangular cross-section typical of windrow turners.

How much compost should I apply?

Typical agronomic rates are 5–20 t/ha (2–9 ton/acre). Apply no more than needed to supply P, because compost is rich in P and repeated applications build up soil P to environmentally problematic levels.

What is the C:N ratio and why does it matter?

The ideal C:N ratio for composting is 25–30:1. Greens (manure, grass, food scraps) are 15:1; browns (straw, wood chips, leaves) are 80:1+. Mix roughly one volume of greens to three volumes of browns. A too-high C:N slows decomposition; too-low C:N produces ammonia odor.

Is compost a substitute for fertilizer?

Compost supplies N, P and K but slowly and at low concentrations (1-3% N, 0.5-2% P₂O₅, 1-2% K₂O). It is best used as a soil amendment to improve structure, water holding and biology, not as the sole nutrient source for high-yield crops.
