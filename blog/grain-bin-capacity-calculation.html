Grain Bin Capacity Calculation: How to Determine Bushels from Diameter and Height

3 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 8 min read

Introduction

Knowing exactly how much grain your bins hold is essential for marketing, logistics, and storage planning. Yet many farmers guess at bin capacity, leading to overfilled bins, inaccurate inventory, and missed marketing opportunities. The good news is that calculating bin capacity requires only basic geometry and your grain's test weight.

This guide explains the cylinder volume formula, how to convert cubic feet to bushels, how peaked and level grain affect capacity, and provides test weight values for major crops. We include a complete worked example for a typical farm bin.

Grain Bin Capacity Calculation: Cylinder Volume Formula

Most grain bins are cylinders. The volume of a cylinder is: V = π × r² × h, where r is the radius (half the diameter) and h is the height of the grain. All measurements must be in the same units — typically feet, giving cubic feet.

For example, a bin 30 feet in diameter and 20 feet tall (to the eave) has a radius of 15 feet. Volume = 3.1416 × 15² × 20 = 3.1416 × 225 × 20 = 14,137 cubic feet. This is the volume up to the eave, not including any peaked grain above the eave.

One bushel (US) is legally defined as 1.2445 cubic feet. To convert cubic feet to bushels: Bushels = Cubic feet ÷ 1.2445. For our example: 14,137 ÷ 1.2445 = 11,360 bushels to the eave.

Accounting for Peaked Grain

When you fill a bin with a center unloading auger, the grain forms a cone above the eave. This cone adds significant capacity. The volume of a cone is: V = (1/3) × π × r² × h_cone, where h_cone is the height of the peak.

The angle of repose for most grains is 20–25 degrees. For a 30-foot diameter bin, a typical peak height is 4–6 feet. Using 5 feet: Cone volume = (1/3) × 3.1416 × 15² × 5 = 1,178 cubic feet = 947 bushels. Total bin capacity = 11,360 + 947 = 12,307 bushels.

If you level the grain (which you should for proper aeration), you lose the cone volume but gain more uniform airflow. Many farmers fill to the eave, level, then add a small peak for maximum capacity while maintaining some aeration.

Grain Bin Capacity: Bushels to Tons Conversion

Bushels are a volume measure, not a weight measure. To convert bushels to tons, you need the test weight (pounds per bushel) of your grain. Standard test weights: corn 56 lb/bu, wheat 60 lb/bu, soybeans 60 lb/bu, grain sorghum 56 lb/bu, barley 48 lb/bu, oats 32 lb/bu.

Actual test weight varies with growing conditions. Drought-stressed corn may test 52–54 lb/bu; high-moisture corn can test 58–60 lb/bu. Always use your actual tested weight for accurate tonnage.

Formula: Tons = Bushels × Test weight (lb/bu) ÷ 2,000. For our 12,307-bushel bin of 56 lb/bu corn: 12,307 × 56 ÷ 2,000 = 344.6 tons. For 60 lb/bu wheat in the same bin: 12,307 × 60 ÷ 2,000 = 369.2 tons.

Worked Example: A 36-foot Diameter Bin of Corn

You have a grain bin 36 feet in diameter with 24-foot eave height. You fill it with corn testing 55 lb/bu at 15.5% moisture. The peak above the eave is 6 feet. You want to know bushels, tons, and value at $4.20/bu.

Step 1 — Cylinder volume: radius = 18 ft. V = 3.1416 × 18² × 24 = 3.1416 × 324 × 24 = 24,429 cubic feet.

Step 2 — Cone volume: V = (1/3) × 3.1416 × 18² × 6 = 2,036 cubic feet.

Step 3 — Total volume: 24,429 + 2,036 = 26,465 cubic feet.

Step 4 — Bushels: 26,465 ÷ 1.2445 = 21,266 bushels.

Step 5 — Tons: 21,266 × 55 ÷ 2,000 = 584.8 tons.

Step 6 — Value: 21,266 × $4.20 = $89,317.

Step 7 — Drying shrink note: at 15.5% moisture, drying to 15% for storage results in approximately 0.59% weight shrink (see moisture shrink article for details).

Common Mistakes and Pro Tips

Using diameter instead of radius. The formula uses radius squared. A common error is plugging the diameter directly into the formula, which gives 4× the correct volume. Always divide diameter by 2 first.

Forgetting the cone/peak. The peaked grain can add 5–10% to bin capacity. If you are calculating for aeration or inventory, decide whether you are calculating level or peaked capacity.

Using standard test weight instead of actual. Test weight can vary 5–10% from standard, especially in drought years. Get your grain tested at the elevator or with a portable moisture tester.

Ignoring core/center support. Some bins have a center support column that displaces grain. Subtract its volume: π × (column radius)² × grain height. For a 2-foot diameter column in our 36-foot bin: 3.1416 × 1² × 24 = 75 cu ft = 60 bushels — small but not zero.

Overfilling for aeration. Grain above the aeration floor's effective zone does not dry properly. Keep peaks modest (3–4 feet) if you plan to aerate, or level the bin completely.

Conclusion

Grain bin capacity is simple geometry: cylinder volume (πr²h) plus cone volume for the peak, divided by 1.2445 to get bushels, multiplied by test weight and divided by 2,000 to get tons. Measure your actual bin dimensions, use your actual test weight, and decide whether you are calculating level or peaked capacity. Accurate bin capacity means better marketing and storage decisions.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate grain bin capacity in bushels?

For a cylindrical bin: Capacity (bushels) = π × (Diameter/2)² × Fill Height (ft) × 0.8038 (bushels per cubic foot). The factor 0.8038 accounts for average grain packing density. Example: A 30-ft diameter bin filled to 20 ft = π × 15² × 20 × 0.8038 = 11,350 bushels. For conical roof space, add the cone volume: Cone Bushels = π × r² × (Cone Height/3) × 0.8038. Use a grain bin capacity calculator for bushels from diameter and height to get instant results, including corrections for grain type, moisture, and packing factors.

How does grain type affect bin capacity?

Different grains have different bushel weights and packing densities: Wheat (60 lb/bu, ~0.80 bu/ft³), Corn (56 lb/bu, ~0.78 bu/ft³), Soybeans (60 lb/bu, ~0.77 bu/ft³), Oats (32 lb/bu, ~0.65 bu/ft³), Barley (48 lb/bu, ~0.72 bu/ft³), Sorghum (56 lb/bu, ~0.78 bu/ft³). Higher test weight grains pack more bushels per cubic foot. The standard 0.8038 factor is an average — for precise calculations, use the specific packing density for your grain. Also, freshly harvested grain settles 2-5% over the first few weeks, increasing effective capacity.

How do I calculate grain moisture shrink?

Moisture shrink accounts for water weight lost when drying grain. Formula: Shrink % = (Initial Moisture % - Final Moisture %) ÷ (100 - Final Moisture %) × 100. Example: Corn at 25% moisture dried to 15.5% = (25 - 15.5) ÷ (100 - 15.5) × 100 = 11.2% shrink. This means 100 bushels at 25% becomes 88.8 bushels at 15.5%. Use a grain moisture shrink calculator for corn and soybean to model drying losses and their financial impact. Factor shrink into your bin capacity planning — wet grain takes more space per marketable bushel.

What size grain bin do I need for my farm?

Calculate: Required Bin Capacity (bushels) = Acres × Expected Yield (bu/acre) × (1 + Storage Buffer %). Then select a bin with capacity ≥ your requirement. Typical bin sizes: 18-ft diameter (~3,000-5,000 bu), 24-ft (~6,000-9,000 bu), 30-ft (~10,000-15,000 bu), 36-ft (~16,000-22,000 bu), 42-ft (~24,000-32,000 bu). Consider: future yield increases, multiple grain types (keep separate), moisture content at harvest (wet grain needs more space), and aeration requirements (don't fill beyond the aeration fan's capacity). Use a grain bin capacity calculator to compare bin sizes and find the most cost-effective option.
