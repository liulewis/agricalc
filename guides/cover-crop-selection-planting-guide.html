Cover Crop Selection and Planting Guide

Mixture Seeding Rate Formula: Species rate in mixture = Monoculture rate × mixture proportion × mixture adjustment factor (0.5–0.75)

18 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 22 min read

Why Cover Crops Matter

Cover crops are plants grown between cash crop seasons primarily to protect and improve the soil, rather than for harvest. They are the cornerstone of conservation agriculture and regenerative farming. Benefits include: reduced soil erosion (up to 90% reduction with good cover), increased soil organic matter (0.01–0.05% per year with consistent use), improved soil structure and water infiltration (20–50% increase in infiltration rate), nutrient cycling and retention (reduced N leaching by 30–70%), weed suppression (reduced herbicide needs), and forage/grazing value. This guide covers cover crop seeding rate calculator and related agricultural calculations to help you optimize your farm operations.

The USDA estimates that cover crops are planted on less than 5% of U.S. cropland, but adoption is growing rapidly (10–15% annually) driven by conservation program incentives (EQIP, CSP), soil health awareness, and yield benefits in water-limited years. The key to success is selecting the right species or mixture for your goals, climate, and rotation, and managing planting and termination correctly.

Step 2: Species Profiles and Characteristics

Grasses (Grass Cover Crops)

Cereal rye (Secale cereale): The most widely planted cover crop in the U.S. Winter-hardy, very cold-tolerant, establishes quickly, produces大量 biomass (3,000–8,000 lb/acre dry matter), excellent erosion control, scavenges residual N (30–80 lb N/acre). Seeding rate: 45–90 lb/acre drilled, 90–120 lb/acre broadcast. Planting: late summer to early fall. Termination: spring, 2–3 weeks before planting (rolling/crimping at anthesis for no-till, or herbicide).

Wheat (Triticum aestivum): Similar to rye but less winter-hardy and less biomass. Good dual-purpose (grain + cover). Seeding rate: 60–120 lb/acre. Oats (Avena sativa): Fast-growing, winter-killed in most climates (zones 7 and colder), good forage, no spring termination needed. Seeding rate: 60–100 lb/acre. Barley (Hordeum vulgare): Similar to oats, more drought-tolerant. Triticale (×Triticosecale): Rye-wheat hybrid, high biomass, good forage.

Legumes (Nitrogen-Fixing Cover Crops)

Hairy vetch (Vicia villosa): The gold standard for N fixation in cold climates. Winter-hardy, fixes 100–200 lb N/acre, viny growth habit, excellent for no-till corn (N released synchronously with corn demand). Seeding rate: 15–30 lb/acre drilled, 25–40 lb/acre broadcast. Needs inoculation (rhizobium leguminosarum biovar viciae). Planting: late summer/early fall. Termination: spring at early flowering (herbicide or rolling).

Crimson clover (Trifolium incarnatum): Fast-growing, showy red flowers, good pollinator value, fixes 70–120 lb N/acre, less winter-hardy than hairy vetch (zones 6+). Seeding rate: 15–25 lb/acre. Red clover (Trifolium pratense): Perennial, very winter-hardy, fixes 80–150 lb N/acre, good frost-seeding candidate. Seeding rate: 8–15 lb/acre. Austrian winter pea (Pisum sativum): Excellent N fixation (100–150 lb N/acre), good forage, less winter-hardy (zones 6+). Seeding rate: 20–40 lb/acre. Berseem clover (Trifolium alexandrinum): Fast-growing, summer annual, great forage and pollinator. Seeding rate: 10–20 lb/acre.

Brassicas (Broadleaf Cover Crops)

Forage radish (Raphanus sativus, also called tillage radish, daikon radish): Deep taproot (12–30 inches) that creates biopores for water infiltration and subsequent crop roots, rapid fall growth, winter-killed, suppresses weeds and nematodes. Seeding rate: 5–12 lb/acre drilled, 10–15 lb/acre broadcast. Planting: late summer (needs 6–8 weeks before frost).

Turnip (Brassica rapa): Similar to radish but more forage value, bulbous root. Seeding rate: 3–8 lb/acre. Rapeseed / canola (Brassica napus): Winter annual in mild climates, high biomass, good weed suppression. Seeding rate: 5–10 lb/acre. Mustard (Brassica juncea / Sinapis alba): Fast-growing, biofumigation properties (suppresses soil-borne diseases and nematodes), winter-killed. Seeding rate: 8–15 lb/acre. Note: Brassicas are NOT nitrogen fixers — they scavenge N but do not add N.

Other Broadleaves

Buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum): Fast-growing summer annual (matures in 6–8 weeks), excellent weed suppressor, pollinator favorite, mines phosphorus, very frost-sensitive. Seeding rate: 40–60 lb/acre. Plant: late spring to mid-summer. Phacelia (Phacelia tanacetifolia): Excellent pollinator cover, fast-growing, cool-season annual, suppresses weeds. Seeding rate: 5–10 lb/acre. Sunn hemp (Crotalaria juncea): Tropical legume, summer annual, fixes 100–150 lb N/acre, high biomass, nematode suppression. Seeding rate: 15–30 lb/acre. Plant after frost danger, needs 8–12 weeks of warm weather. Chicory (Cichorium intybus): Deep taproot, perennial, good forage, drought-tolerant. Seeding rate: 3–5 lb/acre.

Step 3: Designing Cover Crop Mixtures

Single-species cover crops are simple and effective, but mixtures can provide multiple benefits simultaneously (e.g., N fixation + erosion control + weed suppression). The key is to design mixtures with complementary functional traits, not just random combinations.

Principles of mixture design: (1) Include at least one grass (for biomass, erosion control, N scavenging) and one legume (for N fixation) in most mixtures. (2) Limit to 2–4 species — more species increases complexity and cost without proportional benefit. (3) Adjust seeding rates: when mixing, use 50–75% of the monoculture rate for each species (the "mixture adjustment factor"). Total mixture rate is typically 75–125% of the highest single-species rate. (4) Ensure all species in the mix have similar planting dates and termination requirements. (5) Avoid mixing species with vastly different growth habits that will outcompete each other (e.g., aggressive rye with slow-establishing clover — reduce rye rate).

Common proven mixtures: (1) Corn-soybean rotation winter mix: cereal rye (30–45 lb) + hairy vetch (10–20 lb) + crimson clover (5–8 lb) = 45–73 lb/acre. (2) Spring/summer mix after wheat: buckwheat (20 lb) + cowpea (20 lb) + sunn hemp (10 lb) = 50 lb/acre. (3) Forage/grazing mix: oats (40 lb) + field pea (20 lb) + radish (3 lb) = 63 lb/acre. (4) Low-cost erosion mix: cereal rye (60 lb) + oats (20 lb) = 80 lb/acre.

Step 4: Seeding Rate Calculation

Example: Design a 3-species mix of cereal rye + hairy vetch + crimson clover, with 50% rye / 30% vetch / 20% clover by seed weight. Monoculture rates: rye 60 lb/acre, vetch 20 lb/acre, clover 15 lb/acre. Mixture adjustment factor = 0.65.

Rye: 60 × 0.50 × 0.65 = 19.5 lb/acre. Vetch: 20 × 0.30 × 0.65 = 3.9 lb/acre. Clover: 15 × 0.20 × 0.65 = 2.0 lb/acre. Total = 25.4 lb/acre. This is a light mix — for more biomass, increase the adjustment factor to 0.75 or increase the rye proportion.

Alternative approach (simpler): Use "pure live seed" (PLS) rates. PLS = germination % × purity %. Always buy certified seed with a tag showing germination and purity. If seed is 90% germination and 95% purity, PLS = 0.90 × 0.95 = 85.5%. To get 20 lb PLS/acre, you need to plant 20 ÷ 0.855 = 23.4 lb/acre of bulk seed.

Step 5: Planting Methods and Timing

Drilling (grain drill): The most reliable method. Places seed at uniform depth (0.5–1.5 inches), ensures good seed-to-soil contact, uses 20–30% less seed than broadcasting. Best for all species, especially small-seeded legumes and brassicas. Calibrate the drill for each seed size — small seeds (clover, vetch) need a small-seed box or agitator.

Broadcasting: Spreading seed on the soil surface with a spreader, then lightly incorporating (drag, harrow, or light disk) or relying on rain to "plant" the seed. Uses 25–50% more seed than drilling due to lower emergence. Good for large-seeded species (rye, wheat, oats) and for interseeding into standing crops. Must be followed by rain within 3–7 days or seed will die on the surface.

Aerial seeding: Applying seed by airplane or drone into a standing crop (e.g., into corn at V5–V6 or soybeans at R4–R5) before harvest. Allows earlier planting of winter cover crops in short-season areas. Uses 30–50% more seed than drilling. Success depends on timely rain after seeding. Most effective for cereal rye (very tolerant of surface seeding).

Interseeding / relay cropping: Planting cover crops into a standing cash crop 2–6 weeks before harvest. Uses specialized interseeders or high-clearance equipment. Allows earlier cover crop establishment in corn-soybean rotations. Species must be shade-tolerant (rye, annual ryegrass, clover, radish).

Planting timing: Winter cover crops (rye, vetch, clover, wheat): plant 4–8 weeks before first killing frost (late August to early October in northern U.S., October to November in southern U.S.). Summer cover crops (buckwheat, sunn hemp, cowpea, sorghum-sudangrass): plant after frost danger, late spring to mid-summer. Spring cover crops (oats, peas, spring vetch): plant as soon as soil can be worked in early spring.

Step 6: Termination Management

Termination timing is critical — terminate too early and you lose biomass and N benefits; terminate too late and the cover crop competes with the cash crop for water and nutrients, or sets seed and becomes a weed.

Herbicide termination: The most common method. Apply glyphosate (1–2 qt/acre) or paraquat + metribuzin in spring when cover crop is 6–12 inches tall (2–4 weeks before planting). For legumes, terminate at early flowering (10–20% bloom) for maximum N content. For grasses, terminate at boot to early heading stage. Add 2,4-D or dicamba for hard-to-kill broadleaves.

Roller-crimper termination: Mechanical termination for no-till organic systems. Roll cover crop when it reaches anthesis (flowering) — rye at 50% pollen shed, vetch at 100% bloom. Crimping kills the plant by breaking stems, creating a thick mulch. Requires 8,000+ lb/acre biomass for effective weed suppression. Plant cash crop directly into the mulch with a no-till planter/coulter.

Mowing / tillage termination: Mow cover crop and incorporate with disk or plow. Best for green manure (incorporating fresh biomass to release N). Incorporate 1–2 weeks before planting to allow initial decomposition and avoid N tie-up. Winter-killed species (oats, radish, mustard) do not need termination — they die naturally with hard frost.

Key termination rule: Never let a cover crop go to seed. Cereal rye that sets seed can become a volunteer weed problem for 2–3 years. Terminate at least 2 weeks before seed set. For winter cover crops in corn-soybean rotations, terminate 2–3 weeks before planting to conserve soil moisture (critical in dry springs).

Worked Example: Cover Crop Program for 200-Acre Corn-Soybean Farm

A 200-acre corn-soybean rotation farm in southern Michigan (zone 6a). Goals: reduce soil erosion, fix N for corn, suppress weeds, improve soil health. Current program: no cover crops, fall chisel plow. Wants to transition to no-till with cover crops.

Step 1 — Select species for each rotation phase: After soybean (before corn): Need N fixation + erosion control. Mix: cereal rye + hairy vetch + crimson clover. After corn (before soybean): Need erosion control + weed suppression (no N needed — soybean fixes its own). Mix: cereal rye + oats + forage radish.

Step 2 — Calculate seeding rates for post-soybean mix: Mix: 50% rye / 30% vetch / 20% clover. Monoculture rates: rye 60 lb, vetch 20 lb, clover 15 lb. Adjustment factor 0.70. Rye: 60 × 0.50 × 0.70 = 21 lb/acre. Vetch: 20 × 0.30 × 0.70 = 4.2 lb/acre. Clover: 15 × 0.20 × 0.70 = 2.1 lb/acre. Total = 27.3 lb/acre. Round to: rye 20 lb + vetch 4 lb + clover 2 lb = 26 lb/acre. For 100 acres (corn phase): 2,600 lb total seed.

Step 3 — Calculate seeding rates for post-corn mix: Mix: 60% rye / 25% oats / 15% radish. Monoculture rates: rye 60 lb, oats 80 lb, radish 8 lb. Adjustment factor 0.70. Rye: 60 × 0.60 × 0.70 = 25.2 lb/acre. Oats: 80 × 0.25 × 0.70 = 14.0 lb/acre. Radish: 8 × 0.15 × 0.70 = 0.84 lb/acre. Total = 40.0 lb/acre. For 100 acres (soybean phase): 4,000 lb total seed.

Step 4 — Planting plan: Post-soybean (early September): Drill mix at 0.75–1 inch depth immediately after soybean harvest. Use a no-till drill with small-seed attachment for vetch and clover. Inoculate vetch and clover with proper rhizobium before planting. Post-corn (late September–early October): Drill mix after corn harvest. Rye and oats can go through the large seed box; radish through small seed box. Planting date is critical — must be at least 6 weeks before first killing frost (~October 15 in zone 6a).

Step 5 — Termination plan: Spring (mid-April to early May): Terminate post-soybean mix (rye+vetch+clover) 2–3 weeks before corn planting. Apply glyphosate (1.5 qt/acre) + 2,4-D (1 pt/acre) when rye is 8–12 inches tall and vetch is at 10–20% bloom. This maximizes N content (vetch at early bloom = 3.5–4.0% N) while allowing 2 weeks for N mineralization before corn planting. Terminate post-corn mix (rye+oats+radish) 2 weeks before soybean planting with glyphosate only. Oats and radish will have winter-killed, so only rye needs termination.

Step 6 — Cost-benefit analysis: Seed cost: Post-soybean mix: rye $0.30/lb × 20 = $6.00, vetch $2.50/lb × 4 = $10.00, clover $3.00/lb × 2 = $6.00. Total = $22/acre. Post-corn mix: rye $0.30 × 25 = $7.50, oats $0.20 × 14 = $2.80, radish $2.00 × 1 = $2.00. Total = $12.30/acre. Planting cost: $8–12/acre (custom drilling). Termination: included in existing spring burndown herbicide pass (no extra cost). N credit from vetch mix: 80–120 lb N/acre = $48–72/acre savings on N fertilizer. Erosion reduction: 70–90% = avoided soil loss of 2–5 tons/acre/year. Net benefit: $30–60/acre/year, plus long-term soil health improvements. Program pays for itself in year 1 through N savings alone.

Common Cover Crop Mistakes

  • Planting too late in fall: Winter cover crops need 4–8 weeks of growth before killing frost to establish enough biomass and root system to survive winter and provide erosion control. Planting after mid-October in northern climates results in sparse stands, winterkill, and minimal benefit. Use aerial seeding or interseeding into standing crops to get earlier establishment if harvest is late.
  • Letting cover crops go to seed: Cereal rye, vetch, and clover that set seed can become volunteer weeds for 2–3 years. Terminate at least 2 weeks before seed set. For rye, terminate at boot to early heading stage (before pollen shed). For vetch, terminate at 10–50% bloom (before viable seed forms). Mark termination dates on your calendar based on planting date and growing degree days.
  • Not inoculating legume cover crops: Legumes require specific rhizobium bacteria to fix nitrogen. Hairy vetch, Austrian winter pea, and lentils need Rhizobium leguminosarum biovar viciae. Clovers need Rhizobium leguminosarum biovar trifolii. Soybeans/cowpeas need Bradyrhizobium japonicum. Without proper inoculation, N fixation is reduced by 50–90%. Inoculate seed at planting (use a sticker/adhesive) or buy pre-inoculated seed. Inoculant is cheap ($1–3/acre) but the N it provides is worth $50–100/acre.
  • Terminating cover crops too late before planting (moisture competition): In dry springs, a lush cover crop can deplete soil moisture, causing poor germination and early growth of the following crop. Terminate winter cover crops 2–3 weeks before planting in water-limited environments. In wet springs, you can terminate closer to planting (1 week) to maximize biomass and weed suppression. Monitor soil moisture in the top 6 inches — if it is below 50% of field capacity, terminate earlier.
  • Using too many species in a mixture: More is not always better. Mixtures with 5+ species often result in 1–2 dominant species outcompeting the rest, wasting seed money. Stick to 2–4 species with complementary traits. Ensure all species have similar planting dates, growth rates, and termination requirements. A 3-species mix (grass + legume + brassica/broadleaf) is usually optimal for multiple benefits.
  • Planting cover crops without a clear goal: Planting cover crops "because it is good" without defining goals leads to poor species selection and disappointment. Define your primary goal first (N fixation? erosion? weeds? forage?), then select species that excel at that goal. If you have multiple goals, use a mixture. Measure outcomes (biomass production, N content, weed counts, soil health tests) to evaluate success and adjust the program over time.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Cover crop success depends on a systematic approach: define your goal, select the right species or mixture, calculate accurate seeding rates (adjusting for mixture effects and pure live seed), plant at the right time and depth with proper inoculation, and terminate at the correct growth stage with adequate lead time before planting the cash crop.

The most cost-effective cover crop programs use mixtures that combine grasses (erosion control, N scavenging, biomass) with legumes (N fixation) and sometimes brassicas (weed suppression, compaction relief). Start simple with one or two species, measure results, and gradually increase complexity. Cover crops are an investment in soil health that pays dividends through reduced input costs, improved yields, and long-term land productivity. FAQ

When is the best time to plant cover crops?

It depends on the type: Winter cover crops (cereal rye, hairy vetch, crimson clover, wheat) should be planted 4–8 weeks before the first killing frost — typically late August to early October in northern U.S. climates, October to November in southern climates. Summer cover crops (buckwheat, sunn hemp, cowpea, sorghum-sudangrass) are planted after the danger of frost has passed, from late spring through mid-summer, and need 6–12 weeks of warm weather. Spring cover crops (oats, spring peas, spring vetch) are planted as soon as the soil can be worked in early spring. The key is to match the cover crop growing season to the window between cash crops.

How much does it cost to plant cover crops?

Costs vary widely by species and method: Seed cost ranges from $5/acre (cereal rye only, drilled) to $50+/acre (complex mixtures with expensive legumes like hairy vetch). Typical grass-legume mixtures cost $15–30/acre for seed. Planting cost: $8–15/acre if custom drilled, or $0 if you own and operate the drill. Termination: usually $0 extra if combined with existing spring herbicide burndown, or $5–10/acre for a separate pass. Total: $15–50/acre for most programs. However, cost-sharing is available through USDA EQIP ($30–70/acre), CSP, and many state and local conservation programs. With N credits ($50–100/acre from legumes) and erosion prevention, cover crops often pay for themselves.

Can cover crops reduce my fertilizer needs?

Yes, significantly. Legume cover crops (hairy vetch, crimson clover, Austrian winter pea) fix 70–200 lb N/acre, which becomes available to the following crop as the cover crop decomposes. A good hairy vetch stand can replace 80–150 lb N/acre of synthetic fertilizer for corn, saving $48–90/acre at current N prices. Grass cover crops (cereal rye, wheat) scavenge residual soil N (30–80 lb N/acre) that would otherwise leach over winter, releasing it slowly for the next crop. Cover crops also improve nutrient use efficiency by improving soil structure and microbial activity, potentially reducing P and K needs by 10–20% over time. Always soil test to adjust fertilizer rates — do not simply assume a fixed N credit.

Do cover crops increase or decrease soil moisture?

Both, depending on timing. During active growth, cover crops use soil moisture (transpiration), which can reduce moisture available to the following crop if terminated too late — this is the main concern in dry springs and water-limited environments. However, after termination, the residue mulch reduces evaporation from the soil surface by 30–60%, conserving moisture. Over the long term, cover crops improve water infiltration (20–50% increase) and soil water-holding capacity (through increased organic matter), increasing the total plant-available water in the soil profile. The net effect is usually positive for annual water balance, but manage termination timing (2–3 weeks before planting in dry conditions) to avoid short-term moisture competition.

What is the easiest cover crop to start with?

Cereal rye (Secale cereale) is the best starter cover crop for most farmers. It is: (1) very winter-hardy (survives in zone 3+), (2) tolerant of late planting (can be planted as late as early November in many climates), (3) cheap ($0.20–0.40/lb, $10–25/acre seed cost), (4) easy to establish with a drill or broadcast, (5) produces abundant biomass (3,000–8,000 lb/acre), (6) excellent erosion control and weed suppression, (7) easy to terminate with glyphosate in spring. Start with cereal rye at 50–75 lb/acre drilled after harvest, gain experience, then add legumes (hairy vetch, clover) or brassicas (radish) to the mix as you become more comfortable. Oats are another easy option for summer or early fall planting — they winter-kill, eliminating the need for spring termination.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate the right seeding rate for cover crops?

Seeding rates depend on species, planting method, and goal. For drill planting: cereal rye 60-90 lb/acre, crimson clover 15-20 lb/acre, hairy vetch 20-30 lb/acre, radish 5-8 lb/acre. For broadcast: increase rates by 25-50%. For mixes, use proportional rates (e.g., 50% rye + 30% vetch + 20% clover of individual rates). Use a cover crop seeding rate calculator to determine exact amounts based on your acreage, species mix, and planting method. Pure live seed (PLS) percentage matters — adjust for germination rate and inert matter.

Which cover crop species should I choose for my goals?

Match species to your primary goal: nitrogen fixation (legumes: clover, vetch, peas, beans — fix 50-200 lb N/acre), weed suppression (cereal rye, sorghum-sudangrass — allelopathic and fast canopy), erosion control (annual ryegrass, cereal rye — dense fibrous roots), soil compaction relief (forage radish, turnips — deep taproots), forage/grazing (cereal rye, oats, triticale, clover), and pollinator habitat (phacelia, buckwheat, clover). Multi-species mixes (3-5 species) provide multiple benefits but cost more. Consider your climate, planting window, and following cash crop when selecting species.

When should I plant and terminate cover crops?

Planting: after cash crop harvest (late summer/early fall for winter annuals like cereal rye, hairy vetch; spring for warm-season covers like sorghum-sudangrass, buckwheat). Aim for 4-6 weeks of growth before frost for winter annuals. Termination timing depends on the next crop: for no-till planting into cover crop residue, terminate 1-3 weeks before planting (roller-crimper at flowering stage for rye, or herbicide). For incorporation, terminate 2-4 weeks before planting to allow decomposition. Terminate legumes at early bloom for maximum N content. Avoid letting cover crops go to seed if they can become weeds.

How much carbon do cover crops sequester and how do I measure it?

Cover crops sequester 0.2-1.0 ton of CO2 per acre per year (0.5-2.5 t CO2/ha/year), depending on biomass production, root depth, and climate. Above-ground biomass contributes less to long-term sequestration than root biomass — deep-rooted covers (radish, chicory, alfalfa) deposit carbon deeper in the soil profile. To measure: take soil samples at 0-6, 6-12, and 12-24 inch depths before planting cover crops and again after 3-5 years, analyze for soil organic carbon (SOC), and use a soil carbon sequestration calculator to estimate changes. Factors like tillage reduction, cover crop species selection, and residue management all affect sequestration rates.

What are the costs and economic benefits of cover cropping?

Costs: seed $15-$60/acre (single species) to $40-$100/acre (multi-species mix), planting $10-$25/acre, termination $10-$30/acre (roller-crimper once purchased, or herbicide). Total: $35-$115/acre/year. Benefits: reduced fertilizer costs (legumes fix $30-$100/acre N equivalent), reduced herbicide costs ($10-$40/acre from weed suppression), reduced soil erosion (value $5-$20/acre), improved water infiltration (reduced irrigation costs), potential carbon credit payments ($5-$30/acre), and long-term soil health improvements (yield gains of 5-15% after 5+ years). Use a farm budget calculator to track costs and benefits over multiple years — cover cropping often breaks even in 2-3 years and becomes profitable thereafter.

How do cover crops affect soil moisture and water management?

Cover crops can both conserve and consume soil moisture, depending on timing and species. During active growth, they use water (2-6 inches per season) which can be a concern in dry years or before planting a water-sensitive cash crop. However, their residue reduces evaporation from the soil surface by 30-50% after termination, conserving moisture for the following crop. The residue also improves water infiltration by 20-50% (reducing runoff and increasing water stored in the soil profile). In humid regions, cover crops help dry out wet soils for earlier planting. In arid regions, choose low-water-use species (cereal rye) and terminate early. Use an irrigation calculator to adjust water applications based on cover crop residue effects.
