November 2026 WASDE: Corn Falls to $4.60, Wheat to $6.20, Soybeans Rise to $12.10

11 Nov 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 6 min read

Related Calculators: break-even price calculator · farm budget calculator · grain storage calculator

Introduction

On November 10, 2026, at 12:00 PM ET, USDA's World Agricultural Outlook Board released the November WASDE report (No. 677). The headline figures for 2026/27 U.S. season-average farm prices: corn $4.60 per bushel (down $0.10 from October), soybeans $12.10 (up $0.10), and wheat $6.20 (down $0.10 from $6.30). Here is exactly what changed month to month, why it moved, and what the numbers mean for your farm planning — drawn only from the official report.

October vs November: the price table

All figures are 2026/27 U.S. season-average farm prices in dollars per bushel (source: USDA WASDE-677, November 2026):

CropOctober 2026November 2026Change
Corn$4.70$4.60−$0.10
Soybeans$12.00$12.10+$0.10
Wheat$6.30$6.20−$0.10

October's published projections match these October figures exactly, so the month-to-month changes above are the full story — no baseline revisions to note.

Corn at $4.60: a smaller crop, a lower price

USDA cut 2026/27 U.S. corn production to 15,893 million bushels on a yield of 179.6 bushels per harvested acre, down 141 million from October. Ending stocks fell to 1,758 million bushels from 1,849 million, and exports were trimmed to 3,275 million bushels. Even so, the season-average farm price was lowered $0.10 to $4.60, reflecting NASS prices reported to date and expectations for futures and cash prices for the remainder of the marketing year.

Soybeans at $12.10: the only riser

Soybeans were the one crop to gain. USDA raised production to 4,574 million bushels (yield 53.3 bushels per acre) and exports to 1,720 million bushels, while ending stocks tightened to 300 million bushels — down 15 million from October. The tighter balance lifted the season-average farm price $0.10 to $12.10. Soybean meal is forecast unchanged at $340 per short ton; soybean oil is lowered 2 cents to 68 cents per pound.

Wheat at $6.20: soft prices persist

Wheat's season-average farm price slipped a dime to $6.20 per bushel. Domestic use was trimmed by 3 million bushels on lower food use, and ending stocks edged up to 743 million bushels — still 19 percent below last year. With global wheat ending stocks up 4.8 million tons to 280.8 million, the price outlook stays soft — plan marketing around the lower benchmark.

What this means for your planning

Use $4.60 / $12.10 / $6.20 as your 2026/27 planning benchmarks. These are marketing-year weighted season-average projections, not today's cash bid — they represent what USDA expects the whole season to average, which makes them the right anchor for enterprise budgets and break-even analysis. Run your own numbers through our break-even price calculator: if your cost of production sits above $4.60 for corn or $6.20 for wheat, the margin for error is thin — lock in input costs, sharpen your marketing plan, or both. This is information only, not marketing or investment advice.

Conclusion

The November WASDE's message is straightforward: corn and wheat each lost another dime, while soybeans gained one on a tighter balance. The next report lands December 10 — until then, these are the official benchmarks to plan against.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the November 2026 WASDE corn price?

USDA's November 2026 WASDE puts the 2026/27 U.S. season-average farm price for corn at $4.60 per bushel, down $0.10 from the October projection of $4.70.

Did the soybean price change in the November WASDE?

Yes — it rose. The 2026/27 U.S. season-average farm price for soybeans is forecast at $12.10 per bushel, up $0.10 from October's $12.00.

What is the November 2026 WASDE wheat price?

The 2026/27 U.S. season-average farm price for wheat is $6.20 per bushel, down $0.10 from October's $6.30.

Why did USDA cut the corn price when the crop got smaller?

The season-average price reflects NASS prices reported to date and futures and cash price expectations for the rest of the marketing year — not just the size of the crop. USDA cut production 141 million bushels to 15,893 million and ending stocks to 1,758 million, but prices reported so far still point to a lower season average.

Are these the prices I should sell at?

Not exactly. Season-average farm prices are marketing-year weighted projections for the whole season, not today's local cash price. Use them as planning benchmarks and check your local elevator's cash bid before making marketing decisions.
