Irrigation & Water

Crop Water Requirement: From Reference ETo to the Real Irrigation Dose

14 Sep 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 5 min read

Related Calculators: crop water requirement

The question 'how much should I irrigate?' has a precise answer, but it passes through three numbers most farmers have heard and few have used together: reference evapotranspiration, the crop coefficient and effective rainfall. Chain them correctly and the crop water requirement stops being a guess and becomes millimeters you can schedule against.

The Three Numbers Behind Every Irrigation Budget

Reference evapotranspiration (ETo) is the water a standard grass surface loses per day at your location — driven by sun, wind, temperature and humidity, and published daily by weather services and local stations. The crop coefficient (Kc) converts that reference into what your specific crop actually uses at its current growth stage. Multiply the two and you get ETc, the real water use of your field.

The ranges of Kc by stage come from the FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56, the standard reference behind most irrigation planning worldwide: for maize, roughly 0.3 to 0.5 at emergence, rising to 1.05 to 1.2 at mid-season and falling to 0.5 to 0.6 at maturity; fruiting vegetables and sugarcane follow their own curves in the same table family.

Where to get ETo

Use the nearest weather station's published ETo, a local agromet service, or a Class-A pan converted with a pan coefficient. What matters is that the number comes from measured local weather — not from a neighboring region's table.

The Calculation Chain, Step by Step

Step 1: daily crop use

ETc (mm/day) = ETo × Kc for the current stage. During peak demand on a hot week, this is the figure to schedule against; weekly totals are more practical: ETc (mm/week) = ETo weekly average × Kc × 7.

Step 2: subtract effective rainfall

Not every millimeter of rain reaches the root zone — light showers evaporate, intense storms run off. Effective rainfall is commonly taken as 60 to 80% of weekly rain on flat, covered soil, and less on slopes or fresh tillage. Net irrigation requirement = ETc − effective rainfall for the same period.

Step 3: gross dose and pump hours

Systems lose water: drip delivers 85 to 90% of what it pumps, sprinklers 70 to 80%, surface flood methods often 50 to 60%. Gross dose = net requirement ÷ application efficiency. Convert with the two handy equivalences: 1 mm over 1 hectare = 10 cubic meters, so a 45 mm gross dose on one hectare is 450 m3 — and pump time follows from your system's flow rate.

Worked Example: Mid-Season Maize on One Hectare

The local station reports an average ETo of 5.5 mm/day for the coming week. The maize is at mid-season, Kc 1.15, so ETc = 5.5 × 1.15 = 6.3 mm/day, or about 44 mm for the week. Rain recorded and considered effective: 10 mm. Net requirement: 44 − 10 = 34 mm.

The block is sprinkler-irrigated at roughly 75% efficiency, so the gross dose is 34 ÷ 0.75 ≈ 45 mm, which is 450 m3 per hectare for the week. Split into three events, each pass applies 15 mm — enough to refill the top root zone without saturating it. If the pump and mains deliver 20 m3 per hour, each event runs about 45 minutes per hectare. Adjusting within the week

A heat wave raises ETo mid-cycle; a 20 mm storm cancels the next event. The schedule is a rolling calculation, not a calendar printed in spring — update it with every weather shift.

Mistakes and Practical Notes

Using the Wrong Kc for the Stage

Applying the mid-season coefficient to a field at emergence nearly doubles the dose. Keep a simple stage calendar per planting date and move the Kc with it; the crop's actual ground cover is the visual check.

Counting All Rain as Effective

Twenty millimeters in five minutes on fresh-tilled slope is mostly runoff. Underestimate effective rainfall on light showers over dry surface and heavy storms on slopes; overestimate it on gentle, covered ground.

Ignoring Efficiency Drift

Drip tape with clogged emitters or a sprinkler line with worn nozzles no longer delivers its nameplate efficiency. A quick catch-can uniformity test once a season keeps the efficiency number honest.

Irrigation planning services built on FAO-56 methodology all reduce to the same three-step chain used here — measure the atmosphere, correct for the crop, discount the sky and the pipes. Master it once and every crop, season and system becomes a version of the same arithmetic.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between ETo and ETc?

ETo is the water use of a standard grass reference surface at your location; ETc is your crop's actual use after multiplying ETo by the crop coefficient for its growth stage. Scheduling is done on ETc, reported weather gives you ETo.

Where do I find Kc values for my crop?

The FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 56 tables list Kc by crop and growth stage and are the standard behind most extension guidance. Pick the stage your field is actually in, not an average season value.

How much rain counts as effective rainfall?

As a practical rule, 60 to 80 percent of weekly rainfall on flat, covered soil counts toward the crop; reduce it for slopes, fresh tillage, light showers on dry surface and heavy storms that run off.

Should I irrigate the full ETc every time?

Not necessarily. Allowing controlled soil drying between events — replacing a large share of ETc but not refilling to field capacity every cycle — saves water without yield loss in many crops. Adjust to your soil's storage and the critical growth stages.
