Practical Guide to Seeding Rate Calculation

Core Formula: Seeds to plant per acre = Target plant population ÷ (Germination % × Field emergence %)

7 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 13 min read

Why Seeding Rate Matters for Optimal Plant Population and Yield

Seeding rate directly affects yield, crop quality, lodging resistance, and input cost. Too low: wasted sunlight, weed pressure, and yield below potential. Too high: intraspecific competition, lodging (especially in wheat and corn), disease pressure (high humidity in dense canopies), and wasted seed cost. The optimal rate balances these factors and depends on crop, yield potential, soil, planting date, and seed quality.

Seed cost is significant: corn seed $3–6/acre per 1,000 seeds ($100–200/acre at 35,000 seeds/acre), wheat $0.20–0.40/lb ($15–40/acre), soybeans $0.40–0.60/lb ($30–60/acre). Getting the rate right saves money and maximizes yield.

Key Variables in Seeding Rate Calculation

Thousand-Kernel Weight (TKW)

TKW is the weight in grams of 1,000 seeds. It varies by crop, variety, and growing season. Typical ranges: Corn: 250–400 g (dent corn 300–380 g, popcorn 120–180 g). Wheat: 30–55 g (hard red winter 35–45 g, durum 45–55 g). Soybeans: 120–220 g (typically 140–180 g). Barley: 35–50 g. Oats: 25–40 g. Sorghum: 20–35 g. Always weigh a representative sample from your actual seed lot — TKW can vary 15–20% between seed lots of the same variety.

Germination Percentage

Germination % is the percentage of seeds that produce normal seedlings under controlled laboratory conditions (standard warm germination test, AOSA/ISTA rules). It is printed on the seed tag. Certified seed typically has 85–98% germination. Always use the tag value, not an assumed 100%. A 90% germination lot means 10% of seeds are dead or abnormal — you must over-seed to compensate.

Field Emergence / Stand Establishment

Field emergence is the percentage of germinable seeds that actually produce established plants in field conditions. It is always lower than lab germination due to: soil crusting, cold/wet soils (imbibitional chilling), seedling diseases (Pythium, Fusarium), insect damage (seedcorn maggot, wireworm), improper planting depth, and dry soil at planting. Typical field emergence: Corn 85–95% (good conditions), 70–80% (early planting, cold soils). Wheat 80–95%. Soybeans 80–90% (can drop to 60–70% with crusting or seedling disease). Use a conservative value (85–90%) for planning.

Target Plant Population / Density

Target population is the number of established plants per acre you want at harvest. It is determined by crop, yield potential, and management. Typical targets: Corn: 28,000–38,000 plants/acre (irrigated/high-yield 34,000–38,000, dryland 24,000–30,000). Wheat: 1.0–1.5 million plants/acre (30–35 plants/ft²). Soybeans: 100,000–160,000 plants/acre (early planting 120,000–140,000, late planting 150,000–180,000). Grain sorghum: 60,000–100,000 plants/acre.

The Seeding Rate Formula: Calculating Seeds per Acre Accurately

Then convert seeds/acre to weight:

Seed weight (lb/acre) = (Seeds/acre × TKW in grams) ÷ 453,592

Where 453,592 = grams per 1,000 lb (1,000 seeds × 453.592 g/lb). Actually: (seeds/acre × TKW g/1000 seeds) ÷ 453.592 g/lb = seeds × TKW ÷ 453,592.

Metric equivalent: Seed rate (kg/ha) = (plants/ha × TKW g) ÷ (germination × emergence × 100,000)

Adjustments for Planting Date and Conditions

Late planting: Increase seeding rate by 10–25% for crops planted after the optimal window. Late-planted soybeans need higher populations because they have less time to branch and fill canopy. Late-planted wheat (after mid-October in northern hemisphere) needs 20–40% more seed to compensate for reduced tillering.

Early planting / cold soils: Increase rate by 5–10% to compensate for lower field emergence (imbibitional chilling, seedling disease). Use seed treatment (fungicide + insecticide) to improve emergence rather than just increasing rate.

Poor seedbed conditions: If soil is cloddy, dry, or crusted, increase rate by 10–15% and consider re-planting if stand is below 60% of target.

High-yield environments: Irrigated, high-fertility fields can support higher populations (corn 36,000–38,000, wheat 1.3–1.5 million). Low-yield (dryland, low fertility): reduce population to avoid water stress and lodging.

Worked Example: Corn Seeding Rate for 160-Acre Field

Field conditions: 160 acres, central Illinois, deep loam, 180+ bu/acre yield potential, planting April 25 (optimal window). Seed lot: Dekalb DKC61-49, TKW 342 g, germination 96% (from seed tag), seed treatment applied.

Step 1 — Set target population: For high-yield irrigated/rainfed corn in central Illinois, target 34,000 plants/acre at harvest.

Step 2 — Estimate field emergence: Optimal planting date, good seedbed, seed treatment (fungicide + insecticide). Estimate 92% field emergence.

Step 3 — Calculate seeds/acre: Seeds/acre = 34,000 ÷ (0.96 × 0.92) = 34,000 ÷ 0.8832 = 38,497 seeds/acre. Round to 38,500 seeds/acre.

Step 4 — Convert to lb/acre: lb/acre = (38,500 × 342) ÷ 453,592 = 13,167,000 ÷ 453,592 = 29.0 lb/acre.

Step 5 — Total seed needed: 160 acres × 29.0 lb/acre = 4,640 lb total. At 80,000 kernels per bag (typical corn seed bag = ~30 lb): 4,640 ÷ 30 = 155 bags. Or in seeds: 160 × 38,500 = 6,160,000 seeds ÷ 80,000 = 77 bags (correction: typical bag is 80,000 seeds, not 30 lb — seed is sold by seed count). 77 bags × $300/bag = $23,100 total seed cost = $144/acre.

Step 6 — Set planter: 30-inch rows: population = seeds/acre × row spacing (inches) ÷ 12 ÷ 43,560. Seeds per foot of row = 38,500 × 30 ÷ 12 ÷ 43,560 × 12 = 2.21 seeds/ft. Set planter to drop 2.2 seeds/ft (or 5.4 inch seed spacing).

Common Seeding Rate Mistakes

  • Assuming 100% germination and emergence: Always use the seed tag germination % and a realistic field emergence % (85–92%). A 90% germination lot with 85% emergence means only 76.5% of planted seeds become plants — you need 31% more seed than the target population.
  • Using average TKW instead of actual seed lot TKW: Weigh out 1,000 seeds from your actual seed lot (or weigh 250 seeds and multiply by 4). TKW can vary 15–20% between lots, causing 15–20% error in seeding rate if not measured.
  • Planting too deep or too shallow: Corn: 1.5–2.5 inches deep. Soybeans: 1–1.5 inches. Wheat: 0.75–1.5 inches. Planting too shallow causes poor root development and lodging; too deep delays emergence and reduces stand. Check planting depth daily, especially in variable soils.
  • Not adjusting planter for seed size: Corn seed size varies (flat vs. round, large vs. small). Vacuum planters handle most sizes, but plate-type planters need the correct seed plate. Always calibrate the planter with your actual seed before planting — run 100 feet and count seeds dropped.
  • Planting corn at the same population in dryland and irrigated fields: Dryland corn should be 24,000–30,000 plants/acre; irrigated/high-rainfall 34,000–38,000. High populations in water-limited environments increase water stress, reduce ear size, and can lower yield.
  • Ignoring seed treatment for early planting: Early-planted corn and soybeans (cold soils <55°F/13°C) have high risk of seedling disease and insect damage. Fungicide + insecticide seed treatment costs $10–25/acre but can improve emergence by 10–20%, more than paying for itself.

Consider a 50-hectare mixed farm in the Midwest United States implementing this topic. Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Seeding rate calculation is straightforward once you have the four key variables: target plant population, thousand-kernel weight, germination percentage, and expected field emergence. The formula seeds/acre = target ÷ (germination × emergence) then converts to lb/acre using TKW. Use our Plant Population Calculator to get precise results for your operation.

The biggest source of error is using assumed values instead of measured ones. Always weigh your actual seed lot for TKW, read the seed tag for germination, and use a realistic emergence estimate based on planting conditions. Calibrate your planter with the actual seed. Getting the population right maximizes yield and minimizes seed cost — a win for both productivity and profitability.

FAQ

How do I measure thousand-kernel weight?

Count out exactly 1,000 seeds from a representative sample of your seed lot and weigh them in grams. For accuracy, do this 2–3 times and average. Alternatively, weigh 250 seeds and multiply by 4. Use a digital scale accurate to 0.1 g. Always use your actual seed lot, not a published average.

What is the difference between germination and field emergence?

Germination is measured in a controlled lab (warm, moist, disease-free) and represents the maximum potential. Field emergence accounts for real-world stresses: cold soils, crusting, diseases, insects, and improper depth. Field emergence is always 5–20% lower than lab germination. For planning, use 85–92% field emergence for most crops under good conditions.

Can I reduce soybean seeding rate to save money?

Yes, if planting early (before mid-May in northern hemisphere) into good conditions. Research shows that 100,000–120,000 plants/acre can achieve maximum yield in early-planted soybeans, compared to 140,000–160,000 for late planting. However, if stands fall below 80,000 plants/acre, yield loss occurs. Use lower rates only with good seed quality and planting conditions.

How does row spacing affect seeding rate?

Narrower rows (15-inch vs. 30-inch for corn, 7.5-inch vs. 15-inch for soybeans) generally allow slightly lower seeding rates because plants intercept sunlight more efficiently. Corn in 15-inch rows can use 2,000–3,000 fewer seeds/acre. Soybeans in 7.5-inch rows can use 10–15% lower rates. However, the effect is modest — the target plant population is the primary driver.

What if my stand is too low after planting?

Evaluate at V2–V4 for corn, VC–V2 for soybeans. If corn stand is <60% of target (e.g., <20,000 plants/acre when targeting 34,000) and uneven, consider replanting. If 70–80% of target and relatively even, keep the stand — the remaining plants will compensate with larger ears. For soybeans, stands below 80,000 plants/acre with gaps >2 feet may warrant replanting. Replanting cost ($30–50/acre) must be weighed against expected yield gain.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate seeding rate using germination rate and TGW?

Seeding rate (lb/acre) = (Target plants/acre × TGW in grams) ÷ (Germination % ÷ 100 × Expected stand % × 10,000 seeds per 10 grams × 453.6 g/lb). Simplified: Seeding rate (lb/acre) = Target plants/acre ÷ (Seeds per lb × Germination % × Expected stand %). Seeds per lb = 453,600 ÷ TGW (grams). Example: Wheat target 1.2 million plants/acre, TGW = 35g (12,960 seeds/lb), 90% germination, 85% stand: 1,200,000 ÷ (12,960 × 0.90 × 0.85) = 1,200,000 ÷ 9,914 = 121 lb/acre. A seeding rate calculator handles all conversions automatically.

What is thousand grain weight (TGW) and why does it matter?

Thousand Grain Weight (TGW or TKW) is the weight in grams of 1,000 seeds. It determines how many seeds are in a pound or kilogram, which directly affects seeding rate. TGW varies by crop and variety: Wheat: 30–50g (9,000–15,000 seeds/lb); Corn: 250–350g (1,300–1,800 seeds/lb); Soybeans: 130–200g (2,300–3,500 seeds/lb); Barley: 35–50g; Oats: 25–40g; Canola: 3–5g (90,000–150,000 seeds/lb); Alfalfa: 1.5–2.5g. Larger seeds (higher TGW) mean fewer seeds per pound, so you need more pounds per acre to achieve the same plant population. Always use the actual TGW of your seed lot — seed tags list it, or you can count and weigh 1,000 seeds yourself. A seed calculator uses TGW to give precise rates.

How does germination rate affect seeding rate?

Germination rate (from seed tag or lab test) tells you what percentage of seeds will sprout under ideal conditions. If you plant 100 seeds with 85% germination, only 85 will sprout — so you must over-seed by 1/germination to compensate. Formula adjustment: Seeding rate = Base rate ÷ (Germination % ÷ 100). Example: Base rate 100 lb/acre at 100% germination; at 85% germination: 100 ÷ 0.85 = 118 lb/acre. Also account for expected field emergence (stand %), which is typically 80–95% of germinated seed due to soil conditions, insects, and disease. Total adjustment: Seeding rate = Base rate ÷ (Germination × Stand). Never plant seed below 80% germination for field crops — it's false economy. Use a germination calculator to verify your seed quality.

How do I calculate seed cost per acre?

Seed cost per acre = Seeding rate (lb/acre or seeds/acre) × Seed price ($/lb or $/bag) ÷ Seeds per bag (if using bagged seed). Example: Corn seeding rate 35,000 seeds/acre, seed price $250/bag (80,000 seeds/bag): 35,000 ÷ 80,000 × $250 = $109.38/acre. Wheat: 120 lb/acre × $0.25/lb = $30/acre. Soybeans: 140,000 seeds/acre ÷ 2,800 seeds/lb = 50 lb/acre × $0.40/lb = $20/acre. For hybrid vs. OPV (open-pollinated variety): hybrid seed costs 3–10× more but may yield 10–30% more. A seed cost calculator helps compare varieties and seeding rates to find the most economical option for your yield goals and budget.

What is the difference between pure live seed (PLS) and germination rate?

Pure Live Seed (PLS) combines germination and purity into one metric: PLS % = (Germination % × Pure seed %) ÷ 100. Purity is the percentage of the bag that is the desired crop seed (not weed seeds, inert matter, or other crop seeds). Example: Seed tag shows 90% germination, 95% purity: PLS = (90 × 95) ÷ 100 = 85.5%. Seeding rate based on PLS: Rate = Desired pure live seeds ÷ (PLS % ÷ 100). For native grasses and wildflowers, seed is often sold by PLS weight, and rates are specified in PLS lb/acre. For certified crop seed, purity is usually >98%, so PLS ≈ germination, but it's still important to check. A seed calculator can use either germination or PLS to calculate seeding rates.

How do I calibrate a seed drill for the correct seeding rate?

Drill calibration ensures you're actually planting the target rate. Steps: (1) Consult the drill's manual for the setting chart based on seed size and desired rate; (2) Set the drill to the recommended setting; (3) Jack up the drill so wheels turn freely; (4) Measure wheel circumference (or use the manual's calibration method); (5) Turn the drive wheel a known number of revolutions (e.g., 50 revolutions) while collecting seed from each opener; (6) Weigh the collected seed; (7) Calculate actual rate: Actual rate (lb/acre) = Collected weight (lb) × 43,560 ÷ (Area covered in sq ft). Area covered = Wheel circumference (ft) × Number of revolutions × Drill width (ft). (8) Adjust the drill setting up or down and repeat until actual rate matches target within ±5%. A seed drill calibration calculator simplifies the math. Calibrate for each seed lot — TGW and germination vary between lots and varieties.
