Soybean Planting Population and Row Spacing: A Complete Guide

7 Sep 2026 AT 9 min read

Soybeans aremarkably adaptable — they can compensate for low populations by producing more branches and pods per plant, and they can tolerate surprisingly high stands without significant yield loss. But there is still an optimal range, and getting it right saveseed cost while maximizing yield. This guide covers how to choose soybean planting populations, row spacing, and seeding rates based on your field conditions.

How Soybeans Compensate for Population

Unlike corn, which has a relatively fixed ear number per plant, soybeans are highly plastic. A soybean plant grown in wide spacing produces many branches, each with multiple pods. A plant grown in dense stands produces fewer branches but sets pods on the main stem. This compensation means soybean yields are stable across a fairly wide range ofinal stands — typically 80,000 to 200,000 plants/acre (198,000–494,000/ha) in good growing conditions.

However, compensation is not unlimited. Below about 60,000 plants/acre, yields begin to decline because the crop cannot close the canopy fully, losing sunlighto weeds and soil evaporation. Above 200,000 plants/acre, lodging and white mold (Sclerotinia) risk increase, especially inarrows and high-rainfall environments.

Target Final Stands by Environment

Recommended final plant populations vary byield potential and row spacing:

  • High-yield environments (irrigated, >70 bu/acre or 4.7 t/ha): 120,000–160,000 plants/acre (297,000–395,000/ha).
  • Moderate-yield environments (40–70 bu/acre): 100,000–140,000 plants/acre (247,000–346,000/ha).
  • Low-yield / dryland environments (<40 bu/acre): 70,000–100,000 plants/acre (173,000–247,000/ha) to reduce water competition.
  • Late-planted soybeans (after mid-June in Corn Belt): Increase target by 10–20% because late-planted soybeans produce fewer branches.
  • Narrows (15 inches or less): Can use 10–15% lower populations than wide rows because light interception is more efficient.

Row Spacing: Wide, Narrow, and Solid-Seeded

Row spacing is one of the most debated topics in soybean production. Here is how the main systems compare:

30-Inch (76 cm) Rows

The traditional rowidth, inherited from corn production. 30-inch rows allow cultivation for weed control (important in organic systems) and work with existing corn planters. However, canopy closure is slower, and yield potential is typically 2–5 bushels/acre lower than narrows in high-yield environments. Target populations: 120,000–150,000 plants/acre.

15-Inch (38 cm) Rows

The most popular narrow-row system in the U.S. Corn Belt. 15-inch rows close canopy 1–2 weeks earlier than 30-inch rows, improving light interception and weed suppression. Yield advantage of 2–4 bu/acre is common. Requires a dedicated planter or drill. Target populations: 110,000–140,000 plants/acre.

7.5-Inch (19 cm) / Solid Seeding (Drill)

Grain drills plant soybeans in very narrows or solid stands. Thisystemaximizes light interception and is common in northern growing areas (Minnesota, North Dakota, Canada) where early canopy closure is critical. However, it increases white mold risk in high-rainfall areas and makes in-season weed control difficult. Target populations: 100,000–130,000 plants/acre.

The Seeding Rate Formula

To calculate your seeding rate, start withe target final stand adjust for expected field emergence. Soybean field emergence typically ranges from 75–90%, depending on planting depth, soil conditions, seed treatment, and weather.

Seeding Rate (seeds/acre) = Target Final Stand ÷ Expected Field Emergence (%)

Then convert seeds/acre to pounds/acre using seed size (seeds per pound):

Seeding Rate (lb/acre) = Seeding Rate (seeds/acre) ÷ Seeds per Pound

Soybean seed size varies widely — from 2,000 seeds/lb (large-seeded varieties) to 3,500 seeds/lb (small-seeded varieties). Most commercial seed is 2,400–2,800 seeds/lb. Always check the seed tag for seeds per pound or seeds per unit.

Worked Example: 80-Acre Soybean Field in Illinois

A farmer in central Illinois plants soybeans in 15-inch rows following corn. The field has well-drained silt loam, average yield of 62 bu/acre, and the farmer targets 130,000 plants/acre final stand. The seed lot has 2,600 seeds/lb and 92% germination. Expected field emergence is 85% (planted in early May into cool, moist soil with fungicide/insecticide seed treatment).

Step 1: Seeding rate = 130,000 ÷ 0.85 = 152,941 seeds/acre.

Step 2: Converto lb/acre: 152,941 ÷ 2,600 = 58.8 lb/acre.

Step 3: For 80 acres: 58.8 × 80 = 4,704 lb = 78.4 60-lb bags (round to 79 bags).

Step 4: Within-row spacing in 15-inch rows: 43,560 ÷ (1.25 ft × 152,941) = 43,560 ÷ 191,176 = 0.228 ft = 2.74 inches between seeds. Sethe planter accordingly.

Planting Depth and Seed Quality

Soybeans should be planted 1–1.5 inches (2.5–3.8 cm) deep in moist soil. Planting deeper than 2 inches (5 cm) in cool soils can reduce emergence by 10–20%, especially for large-seeded varieties. Planting too shallow (<0.75 inches) riskseedling exposure to herbicides and drying surface soil.

Seed quality matters more for soybeans than for many crops because soybean seed is fragile and can lose viability quickly in storage. Always use seed with at least 85% germination, and consider a fungicide seed treatment for early planting into cold soils. Late Planting and Double-Crop Soybeans

When soybeans are planted late (after wheat harvest in double-crop systems, or after a delayed corn planting), increase the seeding rate by 15–25%. Late-planted soybeans are shorter, produce fewer branches, and have less time to compensate for low populations. For double-crop soybeans in the mid-South, seeding rates of 70–90 lb/acre in 7.5-inch rows are common to maximize canopy closure and yield in the shortened growing season.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Soybean planting populations are more forgiving than corn, but optimizing them still pays. Match your target stand to your yield environment, choose row spacing based on your equipment and disease risk, and always calculate seeding rates based on seed count (not weight) adjusted for expected emergence. Frequently Asked Questions

Q: What is the best soybean population per acre?

A: Most soybeans yield best with a final stand of 100,000–150,000 plants per acre (247,000–371,000 per hectare). High-yield irrigated fields can go up to 160,000, while dryland low-yield fields may do best at 70,000–100,000. Soybeans compensate well, so stands as low as 60,000/acre can still produce reasonable yields.

Q: Does narrow spacing increase soybean yield?

A: Narrows (15 inches or less) typically increase yield by 2–5 bushels per acre compared to 30-inch rows, especially in high-yield environments and northern growing areas. The advantage comes from earlier canopy closure, better light interception, and reduced weed pressure. However, narrows increase white mold risk in high-rainfall regions.

Q: How many pounds of soybeans do I plant per acre?

A: Typical seeding rates are 50–75 pounds per acre, depending on seed size and target population. Since soybean seed size varies from 2,000 to 3,500 seeds per pound, always calculate based on seeds per acre rather than pounds. A 60-lbag of 2,600 seeds/lb contains 156,000 seeds — enough for about one acre at a typical seeding rate.

Q: How deep should I plant soybeans?

A: Plant soybeans 1–1.5 inches (2.5–3.8 cm) deep into moist soil. Deeper planting (up to 2 inches) may be needed in dry soils to reach moisture, but avoid planting deeper than 2 inches in cold soils, as this reduces emergence. Shallow planting (<0.75 inches) risks herbicide injury and soil drying.

Q: Should I increase soybean seeding rate for late planting?

A: Yes. Increaseeding rate by 15–25% for late-planted or double-crop soybeans. Late-planted soybeans produce fewer branches and have less time to compensate for gaps, so a denser stand helps maximize canopy closure and yield. For double-crop soybeans after wheat, rates of 70–90 lb/acre inarrows are common.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the optimal soybean planting population per hectare?

Optimal soybean population is 300,000-500,000 plants/ha (30-50 plants/m²). Early-maturing varieties and narrow rows favor higher populations (400,000-500,000); late-maturing varieties and wide rows use 300,000-350,000. A plant population calculator from row spacing and seed spacing computes exact seeding rates for your configuration.

How does row spacing affect soybean yield?

Narrow rows (15-20 inches / 38-50 cm) typically yield 5-10% more than wide rows (30 inches / 76 cm) due to faster canopy closure, better light interception, and reduced weed pressure. However, narrow rows require specialized drills and may increase white mold risk in high-yield environments. Use 30-inch rows if you need row cultivation for weed control.

How do I calculate soybean seeding rate from target population?

Seeding rate (seeds/ha) = target plants/ha ÷ (germination % × expected field emergence %). Typical field emergence is 85-95%. For 400,000 plants/ha with 90% germination and 90% emergence: 400,000 ÷ 0.81 = 494,000 seeds/ha. A seeding rate calculator by germination rate and TGW converts this to kg/ha.

What is the best soybean seeding depth?

Plant soybeans 2.5-4 cm (1-1.5 inches) deep in moist soil. Deeper planting (4-5 cm) may be needed in dry soils or for early planting, but delays emergence and reduces stand. Shallow planting (<2 cm) risks poor germination in dry surface soil. Ensure good seed-to-soil contact with press wheels.
