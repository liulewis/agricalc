Farm Management & Economics

Farm Bill 2026: Reauthorization Status and Timeline

As of October 2026, Congress has not passed a new Farm Bill — the 2018 Farm Bill continues to operate under extension. But farm policy has not stood still: the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed on July 4, 2025, wrote major commodity-program changes into current law — higher statutory reference prices, a stronger ARC guarantee, new base acres, an extended Dairy Margin Coverage program, and expanded support for beginning farmers. This guide explains what has actually become law, what is still only a proposal, the key dates for the 2025 and 2026 crop years, and what you should do now.

Agricalc Editorial Team 14 min read

Where Things Stand: No New Farm Bill Yet

The short version: no new comprehensive Farm Bill has been enacted. The 2018 Farm Bill (the Agriculture Improvement Act of 2018) remains in effect through congressional extensions. That is why you may hear two seemingly contradictory statements — "the farm bill expired" and "programs are still running" — and both are partly true: authorization timelines have been extended, so programs continue, but no full five-year reauthorization has been completed.

The longer version matters more for your bottom line. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed into law on July 4, 2025, included a package of commodity, dairy, and beginning-farmer provisions that had originally been expected to ride on the next farm bill. Those provisions are now current law — not proposals, not drafts. In practical terms, for ARC/PLC, Dairy Margin Coverage, and beginning-farmer support, the rules have already changed even though no bill carries the "new Farm Bill" label.

This guide covers the farm safety-net programs (commodity support, dairy margin coverage, crop insurance beginning-farmer provisions, and FSA loans). Policy details are evolving; treat this guide as orientation and confirm specifics with official FSA/USDA announcements.

New Statutory Reference Prices Under OBBBA

The statutory reference price is the price floor that triggers Price Loss Coverage (PLC) payments. OBBBA raised these prices by 10–21% across the major covered commodities:

CommodityOld Reference PriceNew Reference Price (OBBBA)
Corn$3.70/bu$4.10/bu
Soybeans$8.40/bu$10.00/bu
Wheat$5.50/bu$6.35/bu
Rice (long grain)$14.00/cwt$16.90/cwt
Peanuts$535/ton$630/ton
Seed cotton$0.367/lb$0.420/lb

A higher statutory price does not mean automatic payments. PLC pays only when the marketing-year average (MYA) price falls below the effective reference price (see the formula below). A higher price floor does, however, mean the safety net engages sooner in a price downturn — which is the entire point of the change.

Stronger ARC: 90% Guarantee and a 12% Payment Cap

Agriculture Risk Coverage (ARC-County) received two upgrades:

1. The guarantee is now 90% of benchmark revenue. ARC pays when actual county revenue falls below 90% of the benchmark revenue (the 5-year Olympic average of county yield × the 5-year Olympic average MYA price). A higher guarantee means payments begin at a smaller revenue shortfall than before.

2. The maximum payment is now 12% of benchmark revenue. In a severe revenue collapse, the per-acre payment rate can reach up to 12% of the benchmark (previously 10%). Payments are made on 85% of base acres, as before.

Together, these changes make ARC-CO meaningfully more responsive to moderate revenue losses — the kind that hurt cash flow without reaching disaster levels.

The New Effective Reference Price Formula

PLC payments are based on the effective reference price, not the statutory price alone. Under OBBBA, the effective reference price is:

Effective Reference Price =
MAX(statutory reference price, MIN(5-year Olympic average MYA price × 88%, 115% × statutory reference price))

Where the 5-year Olympic average drops the highest and lowest of the last five marketing-year average prices and averages the remaining three. The result can float above the statutory price when recent prices are strong, but is capped at 115% of the statutory price.

Worked example — corn: Statutory reference price = $4.10/bu. Suppose the 5-year Olympic average MYA price is $4.60/bu. Then 5-yr Olympic average × 88% = $4.048, and 115% × statutory = $4.715. Effective reference price = MAX($4.10, MIN($4.048, $4.715)) = MAX($4.10, $4.048) = $4.10/bu. The statutory price sets the floor, so the effective price can never fall below it.

Dairy Margin Coverage Extended to 2031

The Dairy Margin Coverage (DMC) program — the dairy safety net that pays when the national milk margin (all-milk price minus feed cost) falls below the producer's selected coverage threshold — has been extended through 2031. Two things to know:

1. Tier 1 coverage limit raised to 6 million pounds. The lower-premium Tier 1 coverage tier now applies to the first 6 million pounds of a dairy operation's production history, letting larger dairies protect more of their milk at the cheaper Tier 1 premium rates.

2. DMC 2026 enrollment uses updated production history. For the 2026 enrollment, production history is based on the highest year of 2021–2023. Dairy producers should confirm their production history figure with FSA before enrolling, because it determines both the coverage volume and the premium.

New Base Acres for the 2026 Crop Year

Starting with the 2026 crop year, 30 million acres of additional base acres become eligible for ARC/PLC payments. Base acres are the historical acreage on which safety-net payments are calculated — many farms have planted acres that were never enrolled as base, or have base that no longer reflects their rotation. The new-base-acre provision expands eligibility, which can change the total payment picture for farms with significant un-enrolled cropland.

Action item: verify your farm's base acres with your local FSA office. Do not assume your base is unchanged for 2026 — check.

Payment Limits

The ARC/PLC payment limit remains $155,000 per person or legal entity, now indexed for inflation — which works out to approximately $164,000 for the 2026 program year. Adjusted gross income (AGI) limits and actively-engaged-in-farming rules continue to apply. Large operations approaching the cap should review entity structure and payment attribution with FSA before the election.

Beginning Farmer Provisions

OBBBA expanded support for beginning farmers in two programs:

1. Crop insurance — extra premium subsidy, tiered by experience. Beginning farmers (generally within their first 10 years of farming) now receive an additional premium subsidy on top of the standard subsidy, scaled by year: +15 percentage points in years 1–2, +13 points in year 3, +11 points in year 4, and +10 points in years 5–10. Eligibility now extends through the 10th year of farming, giving new producers a longer runway of reduced insurance costs while they build equity.

2. FSA down-payment farm ownership loans. The down-payment loan structure: the beginning farmer puts down 5%, FSA finances up to 45% (maximum $300,150) at a reduced interest rate with a 20-year term, and the balance comes from a commercial lender or seller financing. This remains one of the most accessible paths to farm ownership for producers without inherited land or large capital.

What Farmers Should Do Now

1. Verify your base acres. With 30 million new base acres eligible for 2026, confirm your farm's base with FSA — it may change what you're paid on.

2. Run an ARC/PLC election analysis. The 2025 automatic payment required no decision, but 2026 brings back the annual election. The right choice depends on your crops, county yield history, and price expectations. Work through the example below, then run your own numbers with our ARC/PLC Payment Calculator.

3. Check DMC 2026 enrollment (dairy). Confirm your production history (highest of 2021–2023) and enroll during the FSA window.

4. Talk to your local FSA office. Enrollment windows, implementation notices, and payment timing all come through FSA. A short visit or call now avoids a missed deadline later.

5. Beginning farmers: review your options. The extended 10-year crop insurance subsidy window and the FSA down-payment loan terms may materially change your first decade's economics — ask your crop insurance agent and FSA loan officer to run the numbers.

Worked Example: ARC vs. PLC Election Logic

Scenario: A 500-base-acre corn farm is electing for the 2026 crop year. New statutory reference price: $4.10/bu. The 5-year Olympic average MYA price for corn is $4.60/bu. Assume the 2026 MYA price ends at $3.80/bu and the county actual yield is 180 bu/acre, with a 5-year Olympic average county yield of 175 bu/acre and a farm program yield of 150 bu/acre.

Step 1 — Effective reference price: 5-yr Olympic average × 88% = $4.60 × 0.88 = $4.048. Cap = 115% × $4.10 = $4.715. Effective = MAX($4.10, MIN($4.048, $4.715)) = $4.10/bu (statutory floor binds).

Step 2 — PLC payment: Payment rate = $4.10 − $3.80 = $0.30/bu. PLC payment per acre = $0.30 × 150 bu program yield × 85% = $38.25/acre.

Step 3 — ARC-CO payment: Benchmark revenue = 175 bu × $4.60 = $805/acre. Guarantee = 90% × $805 = $724.50/acre. Actual revenue = 180 bu × $3.80 = $684/acre. Shortfall = $724.50 − $684 = $40.50/acre; payment cap = 12% × $805 = $96.60/acre, so the full $40.50 applies. ARC payment per acre = $40.50 × 85% = $34.43/acre.

Step 4 — Compare: PLC pays $38.25/acre vs. ARC-CO $34.43/acre on this farm. On 500 base acres, that's $19,125 vs. $17,213 — a $1,912 difference in favor of PLC under these assumptions.

Takeaway: The election is genuinely close and assumption-sensitive — a higher county yield or a stronger MYA price flips the result toward ARC. That is exactly why the annual election matters: run the numbers for each covered commodity with your county's yield history and your price outlook. Common Misconceptions

1. "A new Farm Bill was passed in 2025." No. What passed was OBBBA's commodity-provision package, signed July 4, 2025. The 2018 Farm Bill remains the authorizing law, operating under extension. Nutrition, conservation, and other titles continue under existing authority — only the commodity/dairy/beginning-farmer provisions described here changed.
2. "Higher reference prices guarantee payments." No. PLC pays only when the marketing-year average price falls below the effective reference price. If prices stay strong, no PLC payment is triggered regardless of how high the reference price is.
3. "I can still apply for Farmer Bridge Assistance." No. Payments were fully disbursed by February 28, 2026, and the program is closed. There is no application window to join.
4. "The $850,000 FSA farm ownership loan limit is available." Not yet — it is a proposal, not law, as of October 2026. Plan financing around current limits and confirm with FSA.
5. "My ARC/PLC choice carries forward automatically." The 2025 crop year was automatic (higher of ARC/PLC), but starting with the 2026 crop year you must elect annually. A choice that was right under old prices may not be right under the new reference prices and 90% ARC guarantee — re-run the analysis each year.
6. "Payment limits were eliminated." No. The $155,000 limit per person/entity remains, indexed to about $164,000 for 2026. Farms near the cap should plan attribution with FSA.

Conclusion

As of October 2026, the farm policy landscape is a hybrid: no new Farm Bill, but substantially new commodity-program law via OBBBA. The practical effects are concrete — reference prices 10–21% higher, an ARC guarantee at 90% of benchmark revenue with a 12% payment cap, DMC extended to 2031 with a 6-million-pound Tier 1 limit, 30 million new base acres for 2026, extended beginning-farmer crop insurance subsidies, and an indexed payment limit near $164,000. The 2025 crop year paid automatically at the higher of ARC or PLC; from 2026, the annual election is back, and the right choice is worth real money — as the worked example shows, assumptions flip the winner.

Policy continues to move: loan-limit proposals may or may not become law, and FSA implementation notices fill in the details. When in doubt, the official FSA/USDA guidance controls. Verify your base acres, run your ARC/PLC comparison with the ARC/PLC Payment Calculator, confirm DMC enrollment if you dairy, and keep your local FSA office's number handy.

Frequently Asked Questions

Has a new Farm Bill been passed?

No. As of October 2026, Congress has not enacted a new comprehensive Farm Bill. The 2018 Farm Bill continues to operate through extensions. However, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), signed July 4, 2025, wrote major commodity-program provisions — higher reference prices, a stronger ARC guarantee, new base acres, DMC extension to 2031, and expanded beginning-farmer support — into current law. So key farm safety-net rules have changed even without a new Farm Bill.

When do the new reference prices take effect?

The higher statutory reference prices (corn $4.10/bu, soybeans $10.00/bu, wheat $6.35/bu, rice $16.90/cwt, peanuts $630/ton, seed cotton $0.420/lb) are written into current law by OBBBA. For the 2025 crop year, payments are calculated automatically at the higher of ARC or PLC with no election required. Starting with the 2026 crop year, the annual ARC/PLC election returns and 30 million acres of new base acres become eligible. Check FSA notices for exact program-year implementation details.

Do I need to sign up for ARC/PLC again for 2026?

Yes. For the 2025 crop year, payments were automatic at the higher of ARC or PLC, so no election was required. Beginning with the 2026 crop year, producers must make an annual election again — choosing ARC-County (ARC-CO), ARC-Individual (ARC-IC), or Price Loss Coverage (PLC) for each covered commodity. Contact your local FSA office during the enrollment window and compare the options for your crops before electing; the right choice varies by crop, county yields, and price expectations.

Can I still apply for Farmer Bridge Assistance?

No. Farmer Bridge Assistance was a one-time payment program, and its payments were fully disbursed by February 28, 2026. The program is now closed and is no longer accepting applications. If you received a payment, it is final; if you did not apply in time, there is no way to apply now. Watch for future FSA disaster or ad-hoc programs through official USDA announcements.

Are the higher FSA loan limits, such as the $850,000 direct farm ownership loan, available now?

No — as of October 2026, the higher FSA loan limits (including proposals for an $850,000 direct farm ownership loan maximum) are proposals only and have not become law. Do not base purchase or financing plans on them. Current FSA loan limits remain in effect; confirm them with your local FSA office before planning a farm purchase.
