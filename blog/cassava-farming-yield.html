Cassava Yield Per Hectare: Planting Density and Tuber Weight

27 Jul 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 9 min read

Related Calculators: cassava yield calculator · fertilizer calculator This guide covers cassava yield per hectare in detail.

Introduction

Cassava (Manihot esculenta), also called manioc or yuca, is the third most important source of calories in the tropics after rice and maize, feeding more than 800 million people. Nigeria is the world's largest producer, followed by Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil. Yet average yields in smallholder systems across sub-Saharan Africa remain stubbornly low at 8–12 tonnes per hectare, compared with 20–30 t/ha in Thailand and 25–35 t/ha under best-management practices. The gap is not genetic — it is agronomic.

This article explains how cassava yield is determined by three multiplicative factors: plant population (density), number of marketable tubers per plant, and average tuber weight. We provide the yield formula, benchmark ranges for major producing regions, worked examples for Nigeria and Thailand, and the specific management practices that drive each component.

Cassava Yield Calculator: Components and Formula

Cassava yield is the product of three components, each influenced by different management decisions:

  1. Plant population (plants/ha) — determined by spacing. Common spacings: 1 m × 1 m = 10,000 plants/ha; 1 m × 0.8 m = 12,500; 0.8 m × 0.8 m = 15,625; 1.2 m × 0.8 m = 10,417. Optimal density is 10,000–15,000 plants/ha depending on variety branching habit and soil fertility.
  2. Marketable tubers per plant — typically 5–12 tubers, of which 3–8 are marketable (>100 g each). Affected by variety, stem cutting quality, weed control, and disease (especially cassava mosaic disease and brown streak disease).
  3. Average tuber weight — ranges from 0.3 kg to 1.5 kg per tuber depending on variety, growing period, and fertility. Long-season varieties (12–14 months) produce larger tubers than short-season (8–10 months).

The yield formula:

Fresh tuber yield (t/ha) = (Plants/ha × Tubers/plant × Avg tuber weight kg) ÷ 1000

Adjust for plant survival rate (typically 80–95%): multiply by survival %.

Dry matter content is 30–35% for most varieties; starch content is 25–32% of fresh weight. For processing into gari, fufu, or starch, dry matter yield is the more relevant metric: DM yield (t/ha) = Fresh yield × DM%.

Worked Example 1: Smallholder Farm, Oyo State, Nigeria

Variety: TMS 30572 (improved, mosaic-resistant). Spacing: 1 m × 1 m = 10,000 plants/ha. Survival: 85% (some stem cutting failure and mosaic disease). Harvest at 12 months. Average 5.5 marketable tubers/plant, average tuber weight 0.55 kg. NPK 15-15-15 applied at 200 kg/ha at 8 weeks after planting.

Calculation:
Effective plant population: 10,000 × 0.85 = 8,500 plants/ha.
Tubers per ha: 8,500 × 5.5 = 46,750 tubers.
Total weight: 46,750 × 0.55 kg = 25,713 kg = 25.7 t/ha fresh tubers.
At 32% dry matter: 8.2 t/ha DM.
At a farm-gate price of ₦150,000/tonne (~$190/t), gross revenue = $4,883/ha.

Without fertilizer and with a local variety, the same farmer might achieve 3.5 tubers/plant at 0.35 kg = 10.4 t/ha — a 60% yield gap. The $50 fertilizer investment generates an additional $2,900 in revenue.

Worked Example 2: Commercial Farm, Khon Kaen, Thailand

Variety: Rayong 9 (high starch, early maturity). Spacing: 0.8 m × 0.8 m = 15,625 plants/ha. Survival: 95% (mechanized planting, quality stem cuttings). Harvest at 10 months. Average 7.2 marketable tubers/plant, average tuber weight 0.65 kg. Fertilizer: 300 kg/ha 16-16-8 formula at planting + 150 kg/ha urea at 2 months.

Calculation:
Effective population: 15,625 × 0.95 = 14,844 plants/ha.
Tubers/ha: 14,844 × 7.2 = 106,877.
Total weight: 106,877 × 0.65 = 69,470 kg = 69.5 t/ha — this is high but within the range for top Thai producers with full irrigation and fertilization. More typical commercial yields are 25–35 t/ha; this example represents an exceptional field.

Realistic adjusted estimate (accounting for non-marketable tubers and harvest losses at 15%): 69.5 × 0.85 = 59 t/ha. At 30% starch content and a starch factory price of $45/t fresh root, gross revenue = $2,655/ha.

Management Practices That Drive Yield

Planting material: Use mature, disease-free stem cuttings (20–25 cm long, 4–6 nodes) from the middle portion of the stem. Cuttings from the top or bottom produce weaker plants. Treat with insecticide/fungicide if cassava mealybug or mosaic disease is present.

Weed control: Cassava is slow to establish and poor at competing with weeds in the first 8–12 weeks. Two manual weedings or a pre-emergence herbicide (atrazine + metolachlor) can increase yield by 30–50% compared with unweeded plots.

Fertility: Cassava responds well to N and K, less to P. A typical recommendation is 60–80 kg N/ha, 40–60 kg P₂O₅/ha, and 80–120 kg K₂O/ha. Apply N and K split: half at planting/early growth, half at 2–3 months. Avoid excessive N late in the season, which promotes top growth at the expense of tubers.

Harvest timing: Tuber bulking accelerates from 4–5 months and continues until 10–14 months. Harvesting too early (6–7 months) sacrifices 30–50% of potential yield. However, delaying beyond 14 months increases woodiness and post-harvest physiological deterioration (PPD), which reduces processing quality.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. Planting too densely. Above 15,000 plants/ha, interplant competition reduces tuber size and increases lodging. Optimal is 10,000–13,000 for branching varieties, 12,000–15,000 for non-branching.

2. Using poor-quality stem cuttings. Old, thin, or diseased cuttings reduce survival by 20–40% and produce weak plants. Always select from healthy, high-yielding mother plants.

3. Delayed weeding. Weed competition in the first 12 weeks can reduce yield by 40–60%. Weed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks after planting, or use pre-emergence herbicide.

4. Ignoring pest and disease. Cassava mosaic disease (CMD) can reduce yield by 20–90%. Use resistant varieties (TMS series in Nigeria, KU50 in Thailand) and rogue infected plants early.

5. Not adjusting for survival rate. Always multiply plant population by actual survival (80–95%). Assuming 100% survival overestimates yield by 5–20%.

Expert Recommendations and Next Steps

Extension specialists from leading agricultural universities consistently recommend a phased approach: (1) Conduct a thorough baseline assessment, (2) prioritize high-impact, low-risk changes, (3) implement on a small trial area, (4) measure results rigorously, and (5) scale based on data. This iterative approach minimizes risk while building practical knowledge specific to your farm's unique conditions.

We recommend consulting your local agricultural extension office for region-specific recommendations, as optimal practices vary significantly by climate zone, soil type, and cropping system. Many extension services offer free soil testing, on-farm consultations, and educational workshops.

Conclusion

Cassava yield is straightforward to calculate: plants per hectare × marketable tubers per plant × average tuber weight, adjusted for survival. The global yield gap — 8–12 t/ha for African smallholders vs. 25–35 t/ha for best practices — is closed primarily through improved varieties, quality stem cuttings, timely weeding, and balanced fertilization. For most tropical smallholders, moving from 10 t/ha to 20 t/ha requires no new machinery, only better agronomy. Calculate your potential yield, identify which component is most limiting, and target that component first.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average cassava yield per hectare?

Smallholder farms in sub-Saharan Africa average 8–12 t/ha. Improved systems with fertilizer and weed control reach 20–30 t/ha. Top commercial farms in Thailand and Brazil achieve 25–35 t/ha, with exceptional fields exceeding 40 t/ha.

What is the best planting density for cassava?

10,000–15,000 plants/ha. Use 1 m × 1 m (10,000) for branching varieties or poor soils; 1 m × 0.8 m (12,500) or 0.8 m × 0.8 m (15,625) for non-branching varieties and fertile soils.

How many months does cassava take to mature?

Short-season varieties: 8–10 months. Medium: 10–12 months. Long-season: 12–14 months. Harvesting before 8 months sacrifices 30–50% of potential tuber yield.

How much fertilizer does cassava need?

Typical recommendation: 60–80 kg N/ha, 40–60 kg P₂O₅/ha, 80–120 kg K₂O/ha. Apply as compound fertilizer at planting and split N/K topdress at 2–3 months.

What is the dry matter content of cassava?

Most varieties have 30–35% dry matter and 25–32% starch. High-starch varieties (Rayong 9, TMS 30572) reach 33–35% DM. Dry matter yield = fresh yield × DM%.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average cassava yield per hectare?

Global average cassava yield is about 12–15 tonnes/ha, but this varies dramatically. Smallholder farms in sub-Saharan Africa typically achieve 5–10 t/ha with low inputs. Commercial farms in Southeast Asia and Latin America regularly achieve 25–40 t/ha with improved varieties, proper fertilization, and weed control. Research trials have recorded yields exceeding 80 t/ha under optimal conditions.

What planting density is best for cassava?

Recommended spacing is 1 m × 1 m (10,000 plants/ha) for most varieties and conditions. In fertile soils with good management, 0.8 m × 0.8 m (15,625 plants/ha) can increase yield. In marginal soils, wider spacing of 1.2 m × 1.2 m (6,944 plants/ha) reduces competition. Plant stem cuttings (setts) at 15–20 cm depth with 2–3 buds facing upward.

How much fertilizer does cassava need?

Cassava responds well to potassium and nitrogen. A general recommendation is 100–150 kg N/ha, 50–80 kg P₂O₅/ha, and 150–200 kg K₂O/ha. Apply half the N and K at planting (4–6 weeks after), and the remainder at 3 months. On acidic soils (pH < 5.5), apply 1–2 t/ha lime. Cassava removes large amounts of K, so maintain soil potassium levels.

When is the best time to harvest cassava?

Cassava is typically harvested 9–12 months after planting, depending on variety and purpose. For fresh consumption (gari, fufu, boiling), harvest at 8–10 months when roots are tender. For industrial starch or flour, harvest at 12–18 months for maximum starch accumulation. Harvest during dry season when soil is loose and roots are easier to pull. Delayed harvest beyond 18 months leads to woody, fibrous roots.
