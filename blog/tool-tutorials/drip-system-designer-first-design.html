Drip System First Design: Sizing Mainline, Laterals and Emitters from a Blank Page

14 Sep 2026 Agricalc Editorial Team 5 min read

Related Calculators: drip system designer

A first drip system looks like plumbing, but it is arithmetic. Five decisions — water need per day, emitter output, lateral length, mainline size and pump pressure — turn an intimidating shopping list into a coherent design that delivers the same drop at row one and row fifty.

Start with Water Need, Not Pipe

Every drip design begins with peak crop water requirement, expressed in millimeters per day or litres per square metre per day. A vegetable block under plastic mulch in mid-summer commonly peaks at 6 mm/day, which is 60 m3 per hectare per day — the volume the system must replace in roughly two irrigation events of three hours each.

Convert volume to emitters per plant using plant spacing. Tomatoes at 0.45 m along the row and 1.0 m between rows occupy 0.45 m2; one 4 L/h emitter per plant delivers peak water in 60/4×0.45 ≈ 6.75 hours per week, or one hour per day. Two emitters per plant halve the runtime and protect against clogging, which is the standard choice for high-value crops.

Choosing emitter output

Low flow (1-2 L/h) suits sandy soils and tight spacing where the wetted bulb stays small; medium (4 L/h) handles most vegetables on loams; high flow (8 L/h) is for orchards with wider root zones. Match the emitter to the soil's lateral spread, not to the catalogue's top seller.

The Design Chain

Step 1: emitters per plant and per hectare

Tomatoes at 0.45 × 1.0 m spacing are 22 222 plants per hectare. With two 4 L/h emitters per plant the peak flow is 22 222 × 2 × 4 = 177 776 L/h during irrigation, roughly 178 m3/h — clearly too much for one zone. Split into four zones of 0.25 ha each and each zone draws 44 m3/h.

Step 2: lateral length and pressure loss

Laterals run along the rows. With 1.0 m between rows, a 0.25 ha zone (50 × 50 m) fits 50 laterals of 50 m each. A 16 mm PE drip tape with 4 L/h emitters every 0.30 m carries about 33 emitters per 50 m. Pressure loss along a 50 m 16 mm PE lateral at this flow is roughly 0.5 bar; together with the 1 bar emitter operating pressure, the lateral inlet needs about 1.5 bar.

Step 3: mainline sizing

The mainline delivers 44 m3/h to the zone. PVC pressure pipe charts give a 90 mm PVC main at 2 m/s velocity for that flow, with pressure loss around 0.3 bar over 50 m. Pump output therefore needs at least 1.5 + 0.3 + 0.2 (valves and fittings) = 2 bar at 44 m3/h — a 3 HP pump on a flat block, 5 HP if there is a 20 m lift.

Worked Example: One-Hectare Tomato Block, Four Zones

Total daily peak demand: 60 m3/ha. Tomato spacing 0.45 × 1.0 m, two 4 L/h emitters per plant. Four zones of 0.25 ha each, irrigated sequentially two times per day at 1 hour each. Per zone: 11 111 plants, 22 222 emitters, peak flow 44 m3/h.

Each zone has 50 laterals of 50 m on a 16 mm PE tape, with emitters every 0.30 m and a lateral inlet pressure of 1.5 bar. The mainline is 90 mm PVC running 50 m from pump to the farthest zone, with head loss of 0.3 bar. The pump delivers 44 m3/h at 2.0 bar; a 3 HP surface centrifugal on a flat block covers it. Filters: a 130 mesh (120 micron) disc filter sized for 50 m3/h to protect the emitters from the silt that clogs them most.

Zoning and runtime

Sequential zones keep peak flow low and pump size small. With four zones each running one hour twice a day, the full cycle finishes in four hours and the pump rests twenty hours — leaving margin for cloudy days when demand falls and nights when no irrigation is needed.

Mistakes and Practical Notes

Buying Pipe Before Computing Demand

Sizing the mainline by guess leads to either expensive over-pipe or chronic under-pressure at the last emitter. Always compute peak flow per zone first, then read the pipe chart for the velocity that keeps friction reasonable.

Ignoring Lateral Pressure Loss

A lateral is not a perfect manifold — friction drops pressure along its length, so the last emitter flows less than the first. Limiting lateral length to keep the loss under 15 percent of operating pressure keeps uniformity above 90 percent, the threshold for high-value drip.

Under-Sized Filtration

More drip systems fail from clogged emitters than from any other cause. Size the filter for double the nominal zone flow so backwash never chokes irrigation, and flush the ends of every lateral weekly during the season.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I size emitters for my crop?

Start from peak water requirement in mm/day and plant spacing; divide the daily litres per plant by the emitter's L/h rating to get runtime. Two emitters per plant halve the runtime and provide redundancy on high-value crops.

What pressure does my pump need to deliver?

Add the emitter operating pressure (commonly 1 bar), the lateral friction loss (typically 0.4 to 0.7 bar at the far end), mainline friction and any lift. Most flat-block drip systems need 1.8 to 2.2 bar at the zone inlet.

How long should each lateral be?

Keep lateral length so that friction loss stays under 15 percent of the operating pressure. For 16 mm PE tape with 4 L/h emitters every 0.30 m, lateral lengths above 60 to 70 m start to lose uniformity.

What filter mesh do I need?

Most drip emitters require 120 to 130 micron filtration, roughly 120 to 150 mesh. Disc or screen filters are both acceptable; the key is sizing for at least double the nominal flow so backwash does not choke the system.
